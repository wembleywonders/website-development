## Programme purpose

Caribbean and wider-diaspora food heritage as three things at once: a craft
to be done well, a body of cultural memory to be preserved and passed on,
and a livelihood. A member should leave able to cook the core repertoire to
a standard they could sell, document family and community food knowledge so
it is not lost when an elder passes, and run a compliant small food
operation.

## Research element

Per `WW-RESEARCH-ELEMENT-BY-PROGRAMME.md`, the Kitchen's research lens —
**food as a survival mechanism operating under conditions specifically
designed to erase identity** — is recorded there as *proposed, not yet
evidenced by real work*. The Baianas do acarajé case (Afro-Brazilian women,
Candomblé-linked, IPHAN-protected intangible heritage) fits the lens but was
deliberately not forced into the single-named-curator format the rest of the
platform's roster uses, because it is a living communal practice, not one
documentable figure — and how it should be represented on the platform is
still undecided.

This syllabus does **not** resolve that. Module 6 teaches the documentation
method and treats the representation of figure-less communal practice as
live, open research — a question a member may contribute to, not a settled
procedure they are taught. Do not "close" this by assigning the Kitchen a
single founding curator in a later revision without real research behind it.

## Badge pathway

Explorer → Builder → Innovator → Leader, consistent with the other
certifying programmes. Explorer is practice and foundational safety;
Builder is competent independent production of the repertoire and a first
documented heritage record; Innovator is originality (own dishes, own
documented tradition, a small trading run); Leader is the capstone — a real
trading event or a substantial Knowledge Commons deposit, planned and
delivered end to end.

## Modules

### Module 1 — Food Safety, Hygiene and the Law

The non-negotiable foundation for cooking for anyone outside your own
household. The 5–63 °C danger zone and why it matters; the 2-hour rule;
cross-contamination and colour-coded practice; personal hygiene and
20-second handwashing; the 14 named allergens and the legal duty to declare
them; registering a food business with the local authority; the Food
Hygiene Rating Scheme (0–5) and what an Environmental Health visit looks at;
and formal certification.

**External anchor:** the content of this module aligns with the **Level 2
Award in Food Safety in Catering** (RQF — the regulated qualification
commonly called the "Level 2 Food Hygiene Certificate"). A member holding a
current certificate may submit it as evidence for the corresponding
assessment criterion (see `assessment-criteria.md`, AAK-1 criterion 1.1).

### Module 2 — Caribbean Culinary Foundations: Rice, Peas and the One-Pot

The core method the cuisine is built on. Full-fat coconut-milk cookery; the
rice-to-liquid ratio and how to adjust it for your own pot and stove;
seasoning the liquid, not the grain; the sealed steam (lid stays on);
toasting rice; the whole-scotch-bonnet technique. Rice and Peas is the
reference dish. Underneath it: knife skills, mise en place, and controlling
heat on a domestic hob.

### Module 3 — Spice, Heat and Marinade: Curry Base and Jerk

Building and blooming a Caribbean curry base (oil, aromatics, curry powder
toasted then bloomed, tomato, liquid, long simmer) and adapting it by
protein. Making jerk seasoning from scratch — scotch bonnet, pimento
(allspice), thyme, scallion, ginger — as a wet paste, and scoring and
marinating by protein and time. The cultural history is taught as content,
not garnish: Caribbean curry arriving with Indian indentured labour and
evolving in the islands; jerk as a Maroon preservation practice developed by
people who had escaped enslavement into the Jamaican interior.

### Module 4 — Regional Traditions and Island Variation

The Caribbean is not one cuisine. The distinct histories of Jamaica,
Trinidad, Barbados and Guyana — African, Indian, Chinese, European and
Indigenous influence combined in different proportions — and how the same
dish diverges: rice and peas with kidney beans (Jamaica) versus pigeon peas
(Trinidad); milder versus hotter curry; flying fish and cou-cou; pepperpot;
doubles and roti. The discipline of specific attribution: name the island
and the tradition, do not flatten everything to "Caribbean".

### Module 5 — Food as Calendar: Celebration, Ceremony and Grief

The foods that mark time. Christmas sorrel and black cake (dried fruit
soaked in rum for months, sometimes years); Easter spiced bun and cheese;
Carnival food; and Nine Night and funerary food as organised community care
— feeding the people who come to sit with a bereaved family. Food as the
element that completes an occasion, and as a way a diaspora keeps a calendar
and a memory when it is far from where the calendar came from.

### Module 6 — Recipe Heritage: Oral Documentation and the Knowledge Commons

Capturing family and community food knowledge before it is lost. Interview
by cooking together rather than interrogating; video capture of the hands
and the whole process, not just audio; converting "likkle bit" and "cook it
till it look right" into measurements and observable cues; recording
technique and the stories that arrive with the food; testing your version
against the source and getting their sign-off.

Introduces the **Knowledge Commons deposit pathway** for documentation that
has value beyond the member's own record: the two-layer consent model
(deposit licence plus a separate, explicit opt-in for WW's own commercial
use), attribution, and the protected-tier screening required when a
documented recipe names a living person who did not themselves consent to be
documented.

**Open research, preserved as open:** how a living communal food practice
that has *no* single documentable figure (the Baianas do acarajé case) is
represented in the Knowledge Commons is an unresolved question. This module
teaches the method and invites Builder+/Innovator members to contribute to
that question as genuine research — it does not present a finished answer.

### Module 7 — Costing, Pricing and the Food Business

The numbers that separate "busy" from "profitable". Ingredient costing per
portion; hidden costs (cooking oil, gas and electricity, packaging, sauce
cups, napkins); food-cost percentage and a 28–35 % target; the "food cost ×
3" pricing floor and when to price above it; a 5–10 % waste factor; and
monthly review as ingredient prices move. Cross-links to TECHreneurs'
pricing work — a member may bring a Kitchen product through the TECHreneurs
pricing gate.

### Module 8 — From Kitchen to Market: Trading, Scaling and the Capstone

Taking food to the public. Choosing a market (food-focused vs general,
indoor vs outdoor, pitch fees, customer type); the legal stack —
registration, public liability insurance, a current hygiene certificate,
hot-holding equipment, a fire extinguisher; display, menu and visible
allergen signage; prepping in a registered kitchen and transporting with
temperature logs; pricing for a market; and running the pitch itself.

**Capstone:** plan and deliver one real trading event — a market stall, a
supper club, or a pop-up — end to end: costed, safety-compliant, with a menu
and allergen matrix, and a written post-event review covering what sold,
margin achieved, and what to change.
