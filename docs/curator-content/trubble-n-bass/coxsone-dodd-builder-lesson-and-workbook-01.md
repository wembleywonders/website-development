## Part One: Learning to Read the Signs

Every act on the list below was signed to a major label, and every one of them made their biggest record after leaving it. That's not a coincidence eleven times over — it's a pattern, and Dodd's whole method depends on being able to spot it before it becomes a pattern.

**Case A — The act nobody scheduled**
A vocal group signed to a major label spent years near the bottom of every release calendar. When the songwriting team who'd given them their one hit fell out with the label, the group's momentum went with them, and the label let them go without much resistance. Freed from the label, the group built their own independent outlet from nothing. Their breakout song started as a melody one member was afraid he'd forget before he got home — built out the same day, with a teenage bass player cutting his first-ever recorded take. It sold past a million copies within weeks.

**Case B — The songwriters treated as replaceable**
A songwriting team left their label after a dispute over how profits were being split. The label sued them and won an injunction stopping them from writing or producing for anyone else — for two years. They spent that forced pause quietly building two new labels. Their first artist under the new banner hadn't originally liked the song they gave her; she was worked through it take by take. Legal restrictions meant the songwriting credit went out under a false name. It still became a hit good enough to reach number one in another country and go gold — the first number-one pop hit that writing team had produced since walking away, years earlier.

**Case C — The geography that ended a partnership**
A label announced it was relocating its entire operation to a new city. One of its longest-running, most beloved groups chose to stay behind in the city they were from — and left the label rather than move. Their new label paired them with a different writing team who built a sound around the lead singer's actual voice instead of the label's house style. The result was their biggest pop hit in six years, in their very first year away.

**Case D — The rivalry that happened without ever leaving**
One member of a vocal group left to go solo — but stayed on the same label. A producer still working with his old group wrote a song widely understood as a direct message to him. His solo career went nearly nowhere for two years. Then one session, built around an early disco groove and his own falsetto, became a genre-defining number one on both the pop and R&B charts.

**Case E — "Other acts' leftovers"**
A group later described their own years at a label bluntly: treated like they lived off whatever the bigger acts didn't use. After roughly seven years, they left. Their new label handed them a song within their first year — but the artist herself thought it didn't fit her, and pushed back on a key detail until it did. The result became the only number-one pop hit of their career.

**Case F — The B-side nobody planned to promote**
A group spent nine years as one of their label's lowest priorities — inconsistent material, inconsistent promotion. A major star at another label personally told them to come with her. Their new producer's first session together included a song that wasn't even meant to be the single — it was the other side of the record. Radio played it anyway until the label had no choice but to make it official. Their first million-selling record, after nine years of being an afterthought.

**What these cases have in common, once you strip the specifics away:**

- The talent was never actually missing. It was already there, unused.
- What changed wasn't the artist — it was someone finally giving them material, attention, or control that actually fit who they were.
- More than one of these breakthroughs happened by accident (a flipped B-side, a song reluctantly reshaped) — which tells you something about how thin the gap between "overlooked" and "undeniable" really is.

## Part Two: Your First Coaching Attempt

This is the Builder-tier requirement itself — everything above was preparation for this. There are two ways to complete it — pick whichever fits where you are right now.

**Path 1 — Organic (default)**

Think of someone else on the platform whose work or ideas you think are genuinely underused — not because they lack ability, but because of something closer to what you just named in Activity 1: wrong material, no real attention, being treated as an afterthought within their own team or project.

1. Have an honest conversation with that person about what you're noticing.
2. Offer one concrete piece of coaching, material, or attention — something specific, not general encouragement. (Compare: Gladys Knight didn't just get "a song," she got a song reshaped around her actual home state once she pushed back.)
3. Write up what happened — what you offered, how they responded, and what (if anything) changed.

**Path 2 — Structured pairing (optional)**

If you don't currently have an obvious person to coach, or you'd rather get one supervised rep in first, book a structured pairing session instead: a scheduled Zoom slot with another member and a facilitator present. Same task as Path 1 — one real, specific piece of coaching, not general encouragement — but the facilitator witnesses it directly rather than you self-reporting it afterward.

Either way, write up what happened — what you offered, how they responded, and what (if anything) changed. If you used Path 2, note the facilitator's name in your write-up; their sign-off stands as the witness record.
