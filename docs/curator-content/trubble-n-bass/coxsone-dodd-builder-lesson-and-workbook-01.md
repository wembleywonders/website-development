## Unit 1 — Learning to Read the Signs (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to name four concrete signs of under-development, in your own words, without retelling any of the six case stories.

Every act in the six cases below was signed to a major label, and every one of them made their biggest record after leaving it. That's not a coincidence six times over — it's a pattern, and Dodd's whole method depends on being able to spot it before it becomes one.

**Case A — The act nobody scheduled.** A vocal group spent years near the bottom of every release calendar. When their songwriting team's relationship with the label soured, the group's momentum went with it, and the label let them go without resistance. They built their own independent outlet from nothing — their breakout song sold past a million copies within weeks.

**Case B — The songwriters treated as replaceable.** A songwriting team left their label after a royalty dispute, got legally barred from working under their own names for two years, and used the forced pause to quietly build two new labels. Their first hit under the new banner — credited under a false name due to the legal restriction — was the first number-one they'd produced since walking away.

**Case C — The geography that ended a partnership.** A label relocated its entire operation to a new city. One of its longest-running groups stayed behind and left rather than move. Their new label built a sound around the lead singer's actual voice instead of the old house style — their biggest pop hit in six years, in their first year away.

**Case D — The rivalry that happened without ever leaving.** One member of a group went solo but stayed on the same label; a rival producer wrote a pointed song about him. Two quiet years followed. Then one session became a genre-defining number one.

**Case E — "Other acts' leftovers."** A group described their own years at a label bluntly — treated like they lived off what bigger acts didn't use. Their new label's first song for them didn't fit until the artist pushed back on a key detail. It became the only number-one of their career.

**Case F — The B-side nobody planned to promote.** A group spent nine years as their label's lowest priority. Their new producer's first session included a song meant only as the B-side — radio played it anyway until the label made it official. Their first million-selling record, after nine years as an afterthought.

What these have in common: the talent was never missing, it was unused. What changed wasn't the artist — it was someone finally giving them material or attention that actually fit. More than one breakthrough happened by accident, which says something about how thin the gap between overlooked and undeniable really is.

## Between-Session Task — no fixed time box

This is the Builder-tier requirement itself. It does not happen inside a lesson session, and shouldn't be forced to.

Carry out your plan from Unit 2:

1. Have the real conversation (Path 1) or attend the facilitated pairing session (Path 2).
2. Offer the specific thing you named — not general encouragement.
3. Note what actually happened, including if it didn't land the way you hoped. (A coaching attempt that didn't work is still real evidence — Dodd's own history includes talent he passed on, not just talent he found.)

Come back to Unit 3 once this has actually happened. There's no deadline built into the lesson itself for this — real coaching attempts happen on real people's schedules, not the lesson's.

## Finding from this stress test (23 Aug 2026)

The 20-minute modular rule does not break here — it needs one documented exception, and that exception already has real precedent elsewhere in WW's pedagogy. Units 1 and 2 (reading, reflection, planning) decompose cleanly into standard 20-minute units exactly like the Pointer rebuild. Part Two's actual coaching action does not, and forcing it into a 20-minute window would be dishonest about what the task really requires — a real conversation with a real person, on that person's schedule, not the lesson's.

**Precedent checked directly, not assumed:** `src/pages/programmes/stemgeneers/curriculum/curriculumData.ts` has a real `duration: 20 | 7` module structure with real `repositoryTask` fields (e.g. "Document your family's skin care knowledge — traditional products, ingredients, purposes...") and `homeschoolingEvidence` arrays per stage. This confirms the underlying pattern is real — STEMgeneers already has real-world tasks a member does outside a timed module, feeding into later work — though the code doesn't use the literal phrase "between-session task." This lesson applies the same underlying device to a Builder-tier relationship-based technique rather than a STEMgeneers repository-seeding task: a genuine second application of an existing pattern, not an invented one.

**Rule confirmed for the template (§2A), captured centrally in `WW-Curator-Course-Content-Templates.md` (item 8):** any Template B lesson's real-world relationship component is a between-session task by default, not a 20-minute unit. Decompose everything up to and including planning/preparation into standard 20-minute units; treat the actual live interaction as untimed between-session work; give it a short, separate return unit for write-up and submission once it's done. Applies to every future Template B build (Kitchener, Kemistry, Vaughan, and the rest), not just Dodd.
