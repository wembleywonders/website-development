## Part One: Four Real Cases

**Case 1 — The band that did it first**

A Cuban-born clarinetist named Mario Bauzá moved to New York in 1930, chasing a jazz scene he'd fallen for on an earlier visit. He found something else there too: in Havana, his dark skin had held him back; in Harlem, he could walk the street free of that same weight. He worked his way into the big-band world, eventually becoming musical director for Chick Webb's orchestra. From there, he set out to build something that didn't exist yet — a jazz big band running on real Afro-Cuban rhythm underneath it, not just borrowing a Latin flavour on top. He brought his brother-in-law up from Cuba to front it, a singer nicknamed Machito. They called the band Machito and his Afro-Cubans — deliberately, publicly naming the African root of the music at a time when almost nobody did. Their 1940s recording "Tanga" is the piece musicologists point to as the actual birth of what's now called Afro-Cuban jazz or Latin jazz.

**Case 2 — The name most people know instead**

Dizzy Gillespie is the name most people associate with Afro-Cuban jazz. He deserves real credit — he's the one who carried the sound outside the tight-knit circle of Latin musicians who'd been playing it for years, and got it in front of audiences who'd never have found it otherwise. But even Gillespie's own history doesn't claim he invented it. That credit belongs to Bauzá and Machito's band, years before Gillespie's more famous experiments. It's worth sitting with that distinction: popularizing something and originating it are not the same credit, and history tends to remember whoever did the first one loudest.

**Case 3 — The collaborator who couldn't read music**

In 1947, Bauzá introduced Gillespie to a conga player named Chano Pozo — self-taught, from one of Havana's poorest neighbourhoods, unable to read or write formal notation, and speaking no English. Some of Gillespie's own bandmates didn't want him there at first. None of that stopped the two of them from building "Manteca" together — Pozo taught it to the band by singing and demonstrating each part directly, since paper wasn't how he worked. He was killed in a bar in Harlem a year later, at 33. His name appears far less often than Gillespie's when this music gets talked about, despite being half of the actual collaboration that made it.

**Case 4 — The brothers behind a dance craze**

Pérez Prado became internationally famous as the "King of the Mambo" in the early 1950s. But the mambo wasn't his invention. The first piece actually titled "mambo" was written years earlier in Cuba by two brothers, pianist Orestes López and bassist Israel "Cachao" López, trying to liven up a slower, older style of ballroom dance. Their innovation reached wider audiences only secondhand, through other musicians who picked it up and carried it forward — Prado among them, well after the López brothers had already built it.

**What these four cases teach, once you strip away the specifics**

- The person history remembers loudest and the person who actually built the thing are sometimes two different people.
- Being first and being famous aren't the same achievement — both are real, but they're not interchangeable, and crediting one without the other misrepresents history.
- The gap usually runs in a specific direction: toward whoever had wider access to mainstream audiences, away from whoever built it inside a smaller community first.
