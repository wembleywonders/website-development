# Trubble n Bass — Artist Development & A&R with Coxsone Dodd
## Explorer Tier, Lesson 1: The Skill Nobody Names

*Tutor: Coxsone Dodd — founded Studio One in 1963, called "the University of Reggae" by Chris Blackwell. Held regular Sunday auditions that discovered Bob Marley and the Wailers among many others. Personally took a young Freddie McGregor into his own home to mentor him — his own words on it: "the greatest experience of my life."*

---

### Before you start — a note on this lesson's shape

Every other tutor in this programme teaches you to make something: a beat, a mix, a melody. You'll walk away from their lessons with a file you can point to.

Dodd's craft doesn't work that way. What he built wasn't a sound — it was *other people's sounds*, ones that didn't exist yet when he found them. Bob Marley walked into a Sunday audition as a raw teenager with something unformed. Dodd's actual skill was recognizing what that unformed thing could become, and then doing the specific, patient work to help it get there. That's a real, teachable craft — but it produces no audio file of its own. It produces *someone else's* better work.

**This matters for how you'll be assessed, and it's worth saying plainly: the platform doesn't yet have a proper way to log this kind of evidence.** Every other technique in this programme has a clean digital trail — a file gets uploaded, timestamped, done. A&R doesn't leave that kind of trail on its own. What you'll be asked to do in this lesson's exercise is closer to what actually verified Dodd's own skill historically: not a recording, but *someone else's account of what happened*, in their own words. Until a better mechanism exists, that account — properly written down, dated, and ideally confirmed by the person you worked with — is the record.

---

### 1. What Dodd was actually listening for

Dodd's Sunday auditions weren't polished showcases — most of what walked through his door was rough. His actual skill wasn't spotting *finished* talent; almost anyone can hear that. His skill was hearing past the rough edges to something specific and real underneath them.

Three things he was documented listening for, distilled into something you can practice:

- **A voice or idea doing something nobody else's does**, even badly. Not "good," *distinct*.
- **A gap between what someone's attempting and what they're actually capable of** — the raw material exceeding the current execution.
- **Whether the person could be worked with.** Talent that can't take direction is a dead end for this specific craft, however good it sounds on day one.

---

### 2. The actual mechanism: audition, then invest

Dodd's method had two separate stages, and conflating them is the most common way people misunderstand A&R:

**Stage one — the audition.** Low-stakes, high-volume, deliberately open to almost anyone. The point wasn't to find someone ready. It was to find someone *worth the time it would take to make them ready*.

**Stage two — the development.** This is where the real craft lives, and where Dodd went further than most: with Freddie McGregor specifically, this wasn't occasional advice — it was sustained, personal, over real time, in Dodd's own home. Real development means showing up more than once, with a specific person, toward a specific improvement — not a one-off compliment or a single piece of feedback.

---

### 3. Why "I told them they were good" isn't evidence

If Builder-tier evidence for this technique is going to mean anything, it has to distinguish real development from being encouraging. Encouragement is free and instant. Development costs time and is specific. A useful test: could you write down, in one sentence, *exactly what changed* in the other person's work because of what you did? "I told them I liked it" fails that test. "I noticed their timing rushed on the second verse, showed them how to count it out loud, and they fixed it on the next take" passes it.

### Try This Yourself (Explorer tier evidence)

1. **Observe, don't yet act.** Watch or listen to two members' raw, early-stage work (with their permission) — anything from this programme, at any tier. For each, write two sentences: what's the *distinct* thing under the rough edges, and what specifically would need to change for it to land.
2. **Write the account.** Since there's no upload mechanism yet for this kind of evidence, write it exactly as if you were Dodd describing a Sunday audition to someone else: who you heard, what you actually noticed, and what you'd want to work on with them if given the chance. Date it. This written account *is* your Explorer-tier evidence — not a placeholder for evidence, the evidence itself, the same way a historian treats a documented account as real when no recording exists.

Builder tier is where you actually do the development work with a real person over multiple sessions — and where the platform's current gap matters most, since that tier's evidence depends on the other person confirming it happened. Until a real submission path exists, keep your own written log current; it can be transferred once one is built.
