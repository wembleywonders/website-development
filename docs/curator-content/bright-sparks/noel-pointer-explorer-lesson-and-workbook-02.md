## Unit 1 — Recap the Method, Then Interest (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to restate the three principles from memory, and explain what interest is for without using the word "interest" in your explanation.

Quick recap of the three principles from Lesson 1:

1. **Start with the question the tool answers, not the tool itself.** Before jargon, state plainly what problem the thing exists to solve.
2. **Ground it in something the learner already has a feel for.** Tie the unfamiliar to something ordinary the learner already half-understands.
3. **Name the mistakes before they happen.** Say the common beginner trap out loud, in advance, rather than letting someone discover it the hard way.

**Interest.** The question first: what does it actually cost, or actually earn you, to have someone else hold or use your money for a while? Grounding it: imagine lending a friend your bike for a month. If they gave it back with a small gift as thanks, you'd feel that was fair — you went without something useful, and got something back for the wait. Interest is that same idea, formalised: a fee for the use of money over time, paid by whoever's borrowing it, earned by whoever's patient enough to lend or save it. The mistake to name in advance: people often treat interest as a small, boring detail sitting off to the side of the "real" number — the price of the phone, the balance on the card. It isn't off to the side. On money you owe, interest quietly turns a manageable debt into a much larger one if it sits unpaid; on money you save or invest, that same mechanism works in your favour instead, turning a small regular amount into something much larger, given enough time. Same mechanism, opposite direction, depending on which side of it you're standing on.

## Unit 2 — Inflation and Credit Scores (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to explain, to someone with zero financial background, why cash sitting still can still lose value, and why having no debt isn't automatically a great credit score.

**Inflation.** The question first: why does the same amount of money buy a little less than it used to, even if you haven't spent any of it? Grounding it: picture a bag of your favourite snack slowly getting smaller over the years while the price stays the same, until one day you notice you're paying more for less. Inflation is the reverse version of that same experience, happening to money itself rather than to a snack — the number in your account doesn't shrink, but what it can actually buy quietly does. The mistake to name in advance: people often treat cash sitting untouched as the "safe" option, because the number never goes down. The number staying the same isn't the same as your money holding its value — it's a different kind of loss, just a quieter one.

**Credit scores.** The question first: how does anyone who's never met you decide whether to trust you with a loan? Grounding it: think of a credit score as a track record, the same way a reference from a previous job tells a new employer roughly what to expect from you before they've worked with you directly. It isn't a measure of how much money you have — it's a measure of whether you tend to do what you said you'd do with money you've already borrowed. The mistake to name in advance: people sometimes assume having no debt at all means having a great score. It's closer to the opposite — a score needs some real history to judge, so no track record at all can leave you looking exactly as untested as someone who's actually missed payments, even though the two situations are nothing alike.

Notice, again, what happened across all three examples in this lesson so far: no formula, no jargon walked in first, and yet each one now has an accurate, ungated explanation a genuine beginner could actually use.
