# Scrap Cat — Resourceful Recovery with George Washington Carver
## Explorer Tier: Finding Value Nobody Else Sees

*(Retrofitted against the §2A modular unit structure, 23 Aug 2026 — see the note at the bottom on why and what changed.)*

*Curator: George Washington Carver — arrived at Tuskegee with no laboratory and built one from the local dump, deliberately teaching resourcefulness by showing students how to build with what was already around them rather than what they wished they had. His Jesup Wagon carried the same principle out to rural farmers: feed hogs acorns instead of bought feed, enrich cropland with swamp muck instead of purchased fertiliser. His technique was never really about any one material — it was about training the eye to see usable value where everyone else sees waste.*

*Core technique for this lesson: resourceful substitution and recovery — finding real, usable material value inside things already being thrown away, rather than buying new. This is Scrap Cat's first Explorer-tier lesson.*

This lesson covers recovering copper from everyday discarded items. **It does not cover, and members should not attempt, disassembling anything that holds refrigerant** — air conditioners, refrigerators, freezers, water coolers, or any other sealed cooling system. Venting refrigerant into the air is illegal everywhere, regardless of which refrigerant type is involved, and this restriction is not optional or a matter of local preference. If a member wants to responsibly source copper from this category of appliance, the correct path is asking a licensed HVAC technician or refrigeration company whether they sell their already-drained units as scrap — never draining or opening a sealed unit personally. **This cap applies at every tier, not just Explorer, until a properly supervised, licensed pathway is designed and approved.**

## Unit 1 — Household and Construction Sources (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to name three household or construction-waste sources of recoverable copper, and roughly where the copper sits in each.

Carver never sent students out to buy the thing they needed. He sent them to look again at what was already sitting nearby, unwanted. That's the whole method behind this lesson: real, recoverable value is sitting inside ordinary household and construction waste, most of it thrown away because nobody looked closely enough to notice it was there.

**Quick, low-effort sources.** A dead microwave has a transformer inside built partly or fully from copper windings, plus a few small motors — none of it hard to access. An old CRT television (the deep, heavy kind, not a flat screen) has a copper yoke around the back of the tube and a degaussing cable wrapped around it, often worth a full fistful once stripped. A dead stove or clothes dryer's power cord is almost always real copper, not aluminium.

**Wire, wherever it turns up.** Any copper wire is recoverable if you're willing to strip it — the thicker the better. Stripping a single extension cord usually isn't worth the time, but construction skips and demolition bins often have offcuts and chunks of much heavier cable thrown in without anyone thinking twice about it.

**Plumbing.** Most residential plumbing has been copper tubing for the better part of a century. As homes get renovated and switched over to plastic tubing, that old copper pipe becomes waste — which is exactly why construction and renovation bins are worth checking. A water heater's flexible connector pipes are copper too, and sometimes get left behind entirely when the unit is swapped out.

## Unit 2 — Motors, Transformers, and Small Electronics (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to explain why a large transformer is the richest single source, and name one small-electronics source.

**Motors and transformers.** Small motors (as long as they're copper-wound, not aluminium) hold real weight in copper — a small one might have half a pound, a furnace-sized one closer to a full pound. Bigger, heavy cast-iron-shelled motors hold more still, but take real time and the right tools to open properly. The single richest source, when you can find one, is a large transformer — the kind found inside an uninterruptible power supply, a utility shed, or equipment powering something that draws serious electricity.

**Small electronics.** CPU coolers from laptops and desktops almost always use some copper, even as manufacturers try to use less of it over time — tight internal clearances mean they can't eliminate it entirely yet.

Notice the shape of what Carver's method is actually teaching here: not "here is a rule," but "here is how to keep looking at ordinary things until the value in them becomes visible." That's a transferable skill, not a fixed list — the specific items will keep changing as materials and manufacturing methods change; the way of looking doesn't.
