# How Streaming & Label Economics Actually Work

## TECHreneurs Module 3: Accounting & Financial Literacy — supporting explainer

### Why this module exists

This isn't music-industry trivia. It's a financial-literacy problem that happens to be easiest to see clearly in music, because the numbers are public and the outcomes are well documented — but the underlying pattern applies to any creative or independent career.

The pattern, over and over: a creator is handed a headline figure (an advance, a contract value, a streaming milestone) and treats it as the answer, when it's actually the start of a question — a percentage of what, recouped against what, over how many years, owned by whom. The gap between the number a creator is told and the number they actually take home isn't usually fraud. It's a structural feature of how these deals are built, and it catches people specifically because nobody taught them to ask the follow-up questions before they signed.

This is exactly why financial literacy has to be built into the Creator's Journey as its own module, not left as something a creator picks up after a bad deal. Career valuation is the habit this module is really teaching: not "how much am I being offered right now," but "what does this look like in 10, 20, 40 years." Creators rarely think in that timeframe naturally — the whole industry is built to keep attention on the next release, the next cheque, the next milestone, not the shape of a whole career.

Two real, ongoing patterns make the long-term stakes concrete without needing to diagnose anyone's health or motives: Earth, Wind & Fire have continued touring with a band whose average age is around 75, and there is widespread public commentary asking why veteran performers — Gladys Knight among them — are still on stage deep into their 80s, visibly struggling in some appearances. Whatever the individual reasons in any specific case, the pattern itself is the lesson: an industry that doesn't build in long-term financial planning produces artists who have no choice but to keep performing indefinitely, because performing is the only income mechanism they were ever given. A creator who understands ownership, recoupment, and long-term valuation from day one is a creator who gets to choose whether to keep performing at 75 — not one who has to.

Everywhere this module uses a real artist's experience below, it's used as an illustration of a principle, not as a verdict on that person's choices or circumstances. The goal is for every WW creator to internalise the questions these examples raise — about ownership, about recoupment, about what a number really means — early enough in their own journey that they never have to learn them the hard way.

### 1. The streaming payout pool

When someone streams a song, the money doesn't go straight to that artist. Instead:

- The streaming platform (Spotify, Apple Music, etc.) collects all subscription and ad revenue into one big pool.
- The platform takes its own cut off the top — often around 30% — before anything else happens.
- What's left is divided among every artist on the platform, based on what share of total streams each artist got that month.
- Whatever share reaches an artist then gets split again — between the record label, the producer, the songwriter(s), and finally the performing artist.

**Worked example:** If an artist's music accounts for 1% of all streams on a platform in a month, they don't get 1% of what listeners paid — they get 1% of the pool that's left after the platform's cut. Then their label, producer, and co-writers all take their agreed percentage of that remaining slice before the artist sees a penny.

By the time it reaches the person who actually made the music, it's commonly a small fraction of the original 1% — often well under a quarter of it.

**Why this matters for pricing decisions:** this is a real example of percentage-of-a-shrinking-pool math — each layer takes its cut from what's left, not from the original total. The same math applies to any multi-party deal (a manufacturer, a distributor, a retailer, a platform fee) — always ask "a percentage of what, at which stage?" before agreeing to a split.

#### 1a. Worked example: same song, three outcomes

Numbers like these make the shrinking-pool effect concrete. Say a songwriter has a 20–25% ownership stake in a song that streams 70 million times, at a rate of roughly $3,000 per million streams:

**Gross royalties generated:** 70 × $3,000 = $210,000

That $210,000 doesn't go to the writer. It first passes through the platform's publisher-payment structure, then through the label's recoupment process (the label pays itself back for everything it spent on the artist — studio time, marketing, videos, advances — before any royalty reaches the artist), then through the split between label, producer, and co-writers.

**What the writer actually took home:** roughly $1,500

That's about 0.7% of the gross figure the song generated — not because anyone was cheating, but because each layer in the chain took its agreed cut before passing the rest down.

Now compare a self-released version of the same math. If that same writer had released the song independently — no label, no recoupment, no label's producer cut — and it only reached 3 million streams (roughly 4% of the label version's reach):

**Take-home:** roughly $10,000

The comparison:

| | Streams | Gross royalties | Writer's take-home |
|---|---|---|---|
| Label-signed | 70,000,000 | $210,000 | ~$1,500 |
| Self-released | 3,000,000 | ~$9,000 | ~$10,000 |

A song with 23x fewer streams, released independently, paid the creator over 6x more than the label-backed version of the same song. This isn't an argument that labels are worthless — reach and resources are real — but it's a sharp illustration of why ownership share can matter more than audience size when it comes to what a creator actually earns.

#### 1b. Advances are loans, not gifts

A record-label advance is often described to artists as a reward for signing. In practice, it works like a loan against future royalties — sometimes described plainly by industry lawyers as "nothing more than a line of credit."

Here's how it actually functions:

- The label pays the artist an advance up front (say, $50,000–$100,000) when the deal is signed.
- The label also spends money on the artist's behalf — studio time, videos, marketing, styling — and adds this to the same running balance.
- Before the artist earns a single pound or dollar in ongoing royalties, the label recoups the advance plus all of these expenses out of the artist's royalty share.
- Only once that full balance is paid back does any royalty money start reaching the artist directly.

**Why this catches people out:** the advance feels like income when it arrives, but it's really the artist borrowing against their own future earnings — at terms the artist didn't set and usually doesn't fully see until well into the deal. An artist can have a genuinely successful, widely-streamed song and still not be owed anything further, because the recoupment balance hasn't been cleared yet.

**Business Law & Contracts / Accounting takeaway:** before treating any advance as income, ask three questions: What exactly gets added to the recoupment balance besides the advance itself? At what royalty rate does repayment happen? What happens to ownership and future terms if the balance is never fully recouped? Same discipline as reading the scope of a 360 deal below — the headline number (the advance) is not the deal; the recoupment terms are.

#### 1c. Why some legacy artists keep earning and others don't: the publishing decision

Everything above is about a single song, a single deal, a single moment of signing. This section is about the other half of the timeline: what happens 20, 40, 60 years later — and why some artists from the same era ended up in completely different financial positions despite comparable fame. None of what follows is about anyone's health, work ethic, or personal choices in later life. It's about one decision, made early or made late: did this artist ever secure a real ownership stake in their own work, and what did they do with it once they had it?

Six real, well-documented artists — spanning six decades of the same industry — show the same principle from six different angles.

1. **Frankie Valli — ownership compounds if you secure it early and hold it.** By the time The Four Seasons disbanded, Valli had secured a share of the publishing rights to the group's catalog. That single decision, made decades before "own your masters" became common industry advice, is repeatedly cited as the foundation of his long-term wealth. A publishing stake doesn't expire — a 1960s hit still earns every time it's streamed, licensed for a film or advert, or performed live. Decades later, the Broadway musical *Jersey Boys* — built on licensing Valli's catalog and life story — became a new, unplanned-for income stream generated entirely on top of a stake he'd already secured half a century earlier. Touring, in his case, reinforces an already-secure royalty base rather than being the only thing keeping the lights on.

2. **Stevie Wonder — ownership can be reclaimed, but only from real leverage.** Wonder's case starts from the opposite position. Signed to Motown at 11 years old, his earnings were held in trust under a contract he had no part in negotiating — and Motown paid him exactly one dollar of the roughly $30 million he'd earned as a minor once he turned 21. What he did next is the lesson: rather than simply renewing, he let the contract expire and renegotiated from a position of undeniable leverage — Motown's own founder later described the negotiation as "the most grueling and nerve-racking we ever had." The resulting deal gave Wonder full creative control, ownership of his publishing through his own company, and veto power over his own releases. The six-year run that followed — *Talking Book*, *Innervisions*, *Songs in the Key of Life* — is widely regarded as one of the greatest creative streaks in music history, and Wonder himself ties it directly to having control, not just money. The lesson isn't "you can always fix it later" — it's that fixing it required real leverage, which meant becoming valuable enough that the label was more afraid of losing him than he was of losing the deal.

3. **Chuck Berry — know what a "credit" actually costs before you agree to it.** Berry's mistake was smaller and more specific than a bad contract — and more universally applicable. Early in his career, he didn't understand that recording royalties and songwriting royalties were separate income streams: "I didn't know that a person got compensation for writing as well as recording a song." On that misunderstanding, a radio DJ who had never written a note was given co-writer credit on Berry's breakout hit "Maybellene" — a payola arrangement dressed up as a courtesy. Berry didn't recover full rights to that song until 1986 — thirty years later — and estimated the lost share was worth roughly twice what he'd actually collected from the song in the meantime. A songwriting credit is not a thank-you. It's a permanent assignment of ownership, and it costs exactly as much as the song ends up being worth — which you can't know in advance. Berry corrected his approach for the rest of a long career and died with an estimated $50 million estate, built largely on the publishing and masters he learned to protect.

4. **Barry White — owning your rights protects the money, but not the promotion.** White prioritized owning his intellectual property early and built his own label and publishing operation while still actively recording — a rare, deliberate strategy for the era. Songs like "You're the First, the Last, My Everything" have generated steady quarterly royalty income for over 40 years as a direct result. But White's story also carries an honest caveat the others don't: when the specific label executive who championed him left the company, label interest in promoting his work visibly cooled — by his own account, costing at least one song a shot at becoming a #1 hit. Owning your rights secures what a successful song earns. It doesn't guarantee a song gets the promotional push to become successful in the first place — that still depends on relationships inside whatever label or platform is doing the promoting.

5. **Donna Summer — winning the fight doesn't mean walking away clean.** Summer filed a $10 million lawsuit against her label and her own manager — who was married to the label's CEO, a direct conflict of interest baked into her management structure from the start. The dispute wasn't only about money; she felt creatively trapped and fought to be released from her contract. She won release — but the settlement still bound her to deliver one further album under the old label's terms, and litigation industry-wide reportedly ran into the tens of millions in legal fees. Her next deal wasn't automatically better either; her new label shelved an album and pressured her to drop her longtime producer. Litigation and renegotiation are real options — but they are slow, expensive, and don't guarantee a clean outcome even when you win. Summer's later approach reframed the lesson usefully: she described treating each song as part of "an asset portfolio" rather than a one-off release, and her estate's continued control of masters and publishing keeps generating income today through streaming, sync licensing, and digital use.

6. **Patti LaBelle — six decades of applying all five lessons at once.** LaBelle's financial position rests on no single dramatic moment, but on consistently applying the other five principles together over a very long career: music royalties, film and TV roles, brand endorsements, real estate, and her own food business, rather than relying on one income stream. She also pushed for equal pay across her entire group early on — a collective financial decision, not just an individual one. Decades into her career, she entered a structured deal monetizing her royalty catalog across 18 studio albums and 47 singles. Even LaBelle's story carries a caution: a former manager successfully sued her, and the settlement now entitles him to a permanent share of royalties and commissions from her ongoing work — a reminder that management relationships, not just label contracts, can create claims against future earnings that last long after the relationship itself ends.

**The arc, put together:** get real ownership, even if it takes leverage to reclaim it later (Wonder). Understand exactly what you're signing away, down to the level of a single writing credit (Berry). Once you have ownership, hold it and let it compound (Valli) — or monetize it deliberately on your own terms when that makes more sense (LaBelle). Know that owning your rights protects your income but doesn't guarantee promotion (White), and that even fighting back and winning has real costs and no guaranteed clean outcome (Summer). No creator needs to make all six mistakes to learn all six lessons — that's the entire point of teaching this as one module, at the start of the Creator's Journey, rather than letting each creator discover it individually, one bad deal at a time.

### 2. The 360 deal — know what you're signing away

A standard record deal used to cover recordings only. A 360 deal covers far more: the label takes a percentage not just of music sales, but of:

- touring and live performance income
- merchandise sales
- publishing (songwriting royalties)
- sponsorships and endorsements
- TV/film appearances

The risk isn't the existence of a 360 deal — it's not reading the scope carefully. An artist who becomes genuinely successful under one of these deals can end up owing a cut of nearly everything they earn from their own name and work, not just their recordings.

**Business Law & Contracts takeaway:** always identify the scope of what a contract claims a percentage of, not just the percentage itself. 15% of "your recordings" and 15% of "everything you ever earn from your career" are entirely different deals with the same headline number.

### 3. Why WW doesn't run a label-style cut

Wembley Wonders' own revenue model is a deliberate contrast to this system:

**The 55/25/20 split (Pardner Hand model):**

- 55% to the creator
- 25% to the community
- 20% to WW CIC operations

Compare that to the streaming/label chain above, where a creator can end up with a small fraction of a shrinking pool after every layer takes its cut. WW's model is built the opposite way round:

- The creator's share (55%) is fixed and comes first, not last after everyone else has taken theirs.
- The remaining 45% is split transparently between two known destinations (community reserve, CIC operations) — not an unlimited number of unnamed intermediaries (label, producer, distributor, platform) each taking an unstated cut.
- The split is auditable by account (Co-op community reserve vs Wise daily operations), so every creator can actually see where the other 45% goes.

This is why understanding streaming/label economics matters even for creators who never sign with a major label: it's the system WW's split is deliberately designed not to replicate.
