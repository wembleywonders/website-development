## Unit 1 — Meeting the Craft Before Meeting the Tutor (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to name, from your own memory of *Red Dwarf*, at
least one moment where a character's flaw — not their strength — is what
made a scene work.

Before you learn Felix Dexter's name, meet the craft in its native habitat.
*Red Dwarf* is a BBC science-fiction sitcom built almost entirely from
characters who are, by design, bad at being the people they'd need to be to
solve their own problems. Nobody on that ship is the aspirational centre. The
comedy doesn't come from one flawed character next to several sensible ones —
it comes from an entire crew of flawed people colliding with each other, with
nobody in the room to play straight man for very long before the joke turns
on them too.

This is the first and most load-bearing of seven principles this module
teaches: **the anti-hero ensemble**. No character needs to be the person the
audience is meant to want to become. Tension comes from the collision, not
from a hero guiding the others toward sense.

**Mini-output — Name the Collision:** Think of one scene you remember from
any long-running sitcom (*Red Dwarf* if you know it, another one if you
don't) where two flawed characters make a situation worse together, and
neither one is clearly "the reasonable one." Describe it in your own words —
what each character wanted, and why their flaws made the scene worse instead
of better.

## Unit 2 — Meeting the Tutor: The Same Craft, Live (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to state, in your own words, three more of the
seven principles and recognise each one in a scenario you invent yourself.

Felix Dexter didn't just admire this kind of writing — he built his own
career practising it. On *The Real McCoy*, he was the ensemble's solo
character-act performer, building whole comic identities out of a single
trait pushed to its limit rather than a rounded, sympathetic backstory. On
*Down the Line*, a spoof of institutional talk-radio, the target was never
softened for balance — the format itself (earnest, self-important, badly
run) was the joke, every episode, without ever pausing to be fair to it.

Three more principles, visible directly in that work:

- **Stakes without solemnity** — a scene can carry real consequence (a job on
  the line, a reputation at risk) without the story stopping to remind you
  how serious that is. The stakes sit in the background; the comedy sits in
  front of them.
- **Stasis as character engine** — a character who never learns, on purpose,
  every single time, is not a writing failure. It can be the entire engine
  of a running bit — the joke *is* that nothing changes.
- **Institutions mocked, not balanced** — satire aimed at an institution
  (a radio station, a council, a court) loses its edge the moment the writer
  tries to also give that institution a fair hearing. Dexter's *Down the
  Line* target was never rescued by a "but to be fair" moment.

**Mini-output — Invent a Static Character:** Invent one original character
(not from any existing show) built entirely around a single trait that never
changes — vanity, stubbornness, insecurity, whatever you choose. Write two
lines of dialogue that show the trait doing the work, with no growth or
lesson attached to either line.

## Unit 3 — The Last Three, and Why This Still Matters (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to state the remaining three principles and
explain, in one sentence, why studying a craft this specific still matters
when comedy looks effortless from the outside.

The last three principles:

- **Genre refusal** — tone doesn't have to stay consistent scene to scene, as
  long as the underlying voice does. A piece can turn from farce to
  something genuinely bleak and back again without needing to pick one lane
  and stay in it.
- **Comedy over unresolved isolation** — real darkness can sit underneath a
  comic premise without ever being resolved by the ending. It doesn't need
  fixing; it can just be the floor the comedy stands on the whole way
  through.
- **Masculinity examined, not modelled** — a comic throughline can be built
  entirely from examining a trait like insecurity, immaturity, or vanity,
  without ever offering an aspirational "healthy" version as the
  counterweight the audience is meant to aim for instead.

Why learn this deliberately, when good comedy can look like it just happens?
Because writers who never name these mechanisms tend to reach for the same
few defaults — a likeable hero, a lesson learned, balance restored by the
end — without noticing they're choices, not requirements. Naming the
mechanism is what lets you choose *against* the default on purpose, the way
Dexter's own work consistently did.

**Mini-output — Spot All Seven:** Pick one scene (real or one you sketch
yourself) and identify, in a short list, which of the seven principles it
uses. It doesn't need to use all seven — most good scenes use two or three
at most. Name which ones, and one sentence each on how.
