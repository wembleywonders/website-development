# Pageturners — Composition Craft with Balla Fasséké
## Innovator Tier, Lesson 1: Retiring a Slot That's Stopped Earning Its Place

*Tutor: Balla Fasséké — 13th-century founder of the Kouyaté griot line, tutor to Sundiata Keita. His actual role in Mande tradition was teaching, not just performing: composed and preserved history for a purpose, in a form built to survive being carried in memory alone.*

---

### Before you start

Builder tier gave you a bank of five formula-slots and an opening stanza. A real griot didn't stop there — a piece long enough to be worth memorizing outgrows its first bank. Some slots keep earning their place across the whole piece. Others were only ever right for the opening and start to feel mechanical the moment the piece moves past where they were built for.

This lesson has two goals: finish the piece your opening stanza started, and learn to recognise — and act on — when a tool that served you well has stopped serving the piece.

---

### 1. Extending without losing the pulse

A longer piece isn't just more lines in the same meter — it has to carry a listener's attention across more time without the pulse ever going slack. The discipline from Builder tier still applies at every line: does this line fit the chosen meter, and does it use a slot from the bank or a deliberate, controlled break from one. What's new at this length is pacing — where the piece needs to move faster (short lines, quick formula turnover) and where it needs to slow down and let a single image sit (a line that deliberately breaks the formula rhythm to make the listener notice).

### 2. Why a slot can go stale

A formula-slot earns its place by doing real work: filling a metrical gap without the listener noticing the seam. But a slot used too often inside one piece stops doing that work — the listener starts noticing the *pattern of the pattern*, which pulls attention away from the subject and onto the machinery. This is the real craft judgment Builder tier's bank-building couldn't teach on its own: knowing when a tool that was right at line four is wrong by line thirty.

### 3. Why this matters beyond this one piece

A composer who never retires a slot ends up with a signature that's really just a habit — every piece sounding like it's wearing the same few joints, whatever the actual subject. A composer who retires and replaces slots as a piece grows keeps the craft in service of what's actually being said, not the other way round. That's the difference between a formula becoming a crutch and a formula staying a tool.

### Try This Yourself (Innovator tier evidence)

1. **Extend the piece.** Continue your Builder-tier opening stanza into a complete short piece (16+ lines), staying in your chosen meter throughout.
2. **Run a retirement pass.** Go through your full five-slot bank from Builder tier. For each slot, judge honestly: did it keep earning its place across the whole piece, or did it start feeling mechanical past a certain point? Retire at least one slot partway through the piece and replace it with a new one, built the same way as before (matched to the same metrical pulse).
3. **Log the retirement decision.** Write two sentences: which slot you retired, at roughly which line, and what specifically told you it had stopped working (not just "I got bored of it" — name the actual effect, e.g. "the listener would start anticipating the shape before the content, which undercut the surprise the third stanza needed").

Submit the complete piece, the bank showing the retirement/replacement, and the two-sentence retirement log together as your Innovator-tier evidence.
