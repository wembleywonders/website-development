## Unit 1 — What Meter Actually Does (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to mark a stress pattern on a real line and explain, in your own words, why a steady pulse helps memory.

You're about to learn a craft that's older than writing. For most of human history, nobody could look a poem up — if it wasn't memorable, it didn't survive. Meter and formula aren't decoration. They're what made an epic *possible* before paper existed.

Meter is a repeating pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. In English, the most common unit is the **iamb** — an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one, like the word *ex-PLORE*.

Say these two lines out loud:

> The morning came and no one saw it rise
> Morning came, nobody saw it rising, though

The first line has a steady, repeating pulse. The second doesn't. Read them again — notice how much easier the first one is to say a second time from memory. That's not an accident. A steady pulse gives your memory something to hold onto, the same way a rhythm helps you remember a phone number better than a random string of digits does.

**This is the actual mechanism griot tradition relies on.** A reciter performing a piece hundreds of lines long isn't remembering *words* — they're remembering a *pattern*, and the words fall into the slots the pattern creates. Break the pattern, and the reciter's memory has nothing left to hold onto.

**Mini-output — Find the Pulse:** Pick any four-line piece you already know by heart — a nursery rhyme, a song chorus, an advert jingle. Mark every syllable that lands with a natural stress, using `/` for stressed and `x` for unstressed, like this worked example:

```
x  /   x  /  x /  x  /
The morn-ing came and no one saw it rise
```

Line 1: ___________________________________________
Line 2: ___________________________________________
Line 3: ___________________________________________
Line 4: ___________________________________________

**Question:** Do the same stress-patterns repeat, line to line? Circle: **YES / NO / PARTLY**

If yes — you've just found the metrical skeleton that makes it memorable. If no or partly — that's fine too; noticing the *absence* of a pattern is just as useful a skill as spotting one.

**Consolidation moment (≈2 min):** Without looking back at Unit 1, explain out loud or in writing why a steady pulse helps a reciter's memory. If you can't, re-read before continuing.

## Unit 2 — What Formula Actually Does (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to build one reusable formula-slot and use it correctly on two different subjects.

A formula is a reusable phrase-unit that fits a specific metrical slot and can be dropped into many different lines without breaking the rhythm. Homer's "the wine-dark sea" is the most famous example in the Western tradition — not because it's the most beautiful possible description of the ocean, but because it *fits the meter exactly*, every single time, so a performer never has to stop and invent a description mid-recitation.

Three formula-slots — reusable phrase shapes you could drop into different lines about different people:

- **[name], [epithet]** — e.g. "Sundiata, lion of Mali" / "Marcus, keeper of the block"
- **as [natural image], so [human quality]** — e.g. "as the river carves stone, so patience wears down doubt"
- **[action verb], [action verb], and [consequence]** — e.g. "he searched, he waited, and the answer came"

Notice: none of these are about *this specific person or event*. They're shapes you can reuse for *any* person or event that fits the pattern. That reusability is the entire point — it's what let a griot compose new material about a new patron without inventing the whole craft from scratch each time.

**Mini-output — Build Your Formula-Slot:** Choose ONE of the three shapes above.

Which one did you choose? _______________________________

Use it TWICE, about two completely different subjects — a person you know, a place, a memory, anything. Keep the exact shape identical both times; only the content changes.

Use #1: ____________________________________________
Use #2: ____________________________________________

**Self-check:** Read both lines out loud back to back. Does the shape genuinely feel the same, even though the content is different? Circle: **YES / NO**

If no, look at where they diverge — try once more if needed: Use #3 (retry): ____________________________________

**Consolidation moment (≈2 min):** Without looking back, state the general shape of the formula-slot you built (not your specific examples — the underlying pattern). If you can only recall your examples and not the shape, that's worth another pass.

## Unit 3 — Why This Still Matters, and Applying It (≈20 min)

**Mini-objective:** be able to explain why meter/formula matter even with paper available, demonstrated by directly comparing a broken version against the original.

You have paper. You don't need memory the way a 13th-century griot did. So why learn this at all?

Because meter and formula don't just help memory — **they shape what gets said.** A tight metrical pattern forces economy: you can't ramble, because the pattern won't let you. A formula-slot forces you to think about *function* before *decoration*: what is this phrase actually *doing* in the line, not just how does it sound. Writers who never learn this tend to write loose, over-explained lines with no internal pressure holding them together. Writers who learn it — even if they later break every rule on purpose — write with a kind of compression that's hard to fake.

**Mini-output — Break It on Purpose:** Take Unit 1's original four lines and rewrite them so the meter is deliberately broken — add or remove syllables randomly, ignore the pattern entirely.

Broken Line 1: _____________________________________
Broken Line 2: _____________________________________
Broken Line 3: _____________________________________
Broken Line 4: _____________________________________

Try to say your broken version from memory after reading it twice. Then try the original again from memory after reading it twice.

**Which was easier to hold in your head?** Circle: **ORIGINAL / BROKEN**

Write one sentence on why, in your own words:

_____________________________________________________

(If you answered "broken" — that's a genuinely interesting result, not a wrong one. It might mean you respond to a different memory mechanism than pure stress-pattern, worth knowing about your own process going forward.)
