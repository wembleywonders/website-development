# Pageturners — Composition Craft with Balla Fasséké
## Builder Tier, Lesson 1: Composing With a Bank

*Tutor: Balla Fasséké — 13th-century founder of the Kouyaté griot line, tutor to Sundiata Keita. His actual role in Mande tradition was teaching, not just performing: composed and preserved history for a purpose, in a form built to survive being carried in memory alone.*

---

### Before you start

Explorer tier taught you two separate mechanisms in isolation: spotting a metrical pulse, and building one reusable formula-slot. That's necessary but not sufficient — a griot didn't perform with one formula-slot. They performed with a working *bank* of them, matched to a chosen meter, deep enough to carry a piece longer than any single memorized sentence.

This lesson has one goal: take you from "I can build one formula-slot" to "I can choose a meter for my own subject and build enough slots to compose something worth remembering." That's the actual jump from apprentice exercise to working craft.

---

### 1. Choosing a meter for your subject, not by default

Explorer tier used the iamb because it's the easiest pulse to hear in English. But a real composer chooses meter *for* the subject, not out of habit. A steady, unbroken iambic pulse suits praise or narrative that should feel inevitable. A meter with a hard stop — a trochee (stressed-then-unstressed, like *TROU-ble*) landing at the end of a line — suits a warning, or a line meant to land like a verdict.

Before building anything else, name your subject in one sentence, then ask: should this feel inevitable, urgent, mournful, or triumphant? That answer should decide your meter, not the other way round.

---

### 2. Building a bank, not a single slot

One formula-slot proves the mechanism works. A bank of five or more, all matched to the same chosen meter, is what actually lets you compose something long. The discipline here is consistency: every slot in your bank has to fit the *same* metrical shape, so you can move between them without the reciter's memory losing its footing.

Worked example, all in the same meter as Explorer tier's "[name], [epithet]" slot:
- **[name], [epithet]** — "Sundiata, lion of Mali"
- **[name], who [past action]** — "Sundiata, who walked when no one believed he would"
- **before [name], [state]; after [name], [changed state]** — "before Sundiata, the grass kingdom; after Sundiata, the iron kingdom"

Notice these aren't random — they're built so any one of them could follow any other without breaking the pulse. That interchangeability is the actual engineering behind a long oral composition.

---

### 3. Why this still matters when you can just write it down

At Explorer tier you learned meter and formula shape what gets said, not just how memorable it is. At Builder tier, the same discipline applies at a larger scale: a bank of consistent slots forces *structural* economy across an entire piece, not just line-by-line compression. Writers who skip this step tend to produce pieces where the opening and closing don't feel like they belong to the same voice. A real bank, built and used deliberately, is what keeps a longer piece coherent.

### Try This Yourself (Builder tier evidence)

1. **Choose your meter deliberately.** Name your subject in one sentence, name the feeling it should land as, and state which meter you're choosing and why — one sentence is enough.
2. **Build a bank of at least five formula-slots**, all matched to that meter, following the worked-example pattern above (varied shapes, same underlying pulse).
3. **Draft an opening stanza** (4–8 lines) of an original piece using at least three slots from your bank, in your chosen meter.

Submit the meter choice, the five-slot bank, and the opening stanza together as your Builder-tier evidence.
