# WW-SPEC-SOURCE-VETTING-RUBRIC-001

**The Four-Test Source Vetting Rubric**

**Status:** Canonical — first formal write-up · **Date:** 3 Sept 2026

---

## 1. Origin and status

This rubric has been referenced across multiple WW specs — including
WW-SPEC-THESE-ARE-THE-FACTS-001, which triggers directly off it — but has
never existed as its own document. It was built directly from Rayd-yo's four
constitutional journalistic tests and has been applied, informally, as a
pre-production filter run against archived/found video before any material is
used in WW content.

This document formalises it. It is the canonical source; any other document
referencing "the four-test rubric" should point here rather than restating the
tests inline.

Distinct from, but related to, Rayd-yo's own on-air use of the same four tests
against live guests/claims — that's the constitutional pillar governing live
conversation; this rubric is its pre-production/content-vetting counterpart, and
now also the trigger condition for WW-SPEC-THESE-ARE-THE-FACTS-001's real-time
correction mechanism.

## 2. The four tests

Applied per-claim within a piece of content — a single video, article, or
segment can be part-pass, part-fail; the rubric does not require an
all-or-nothing verdict at the claim level.

**Test 1 — Source every claim.** Pass: the claim is traceable to a named
person, primary text, dated event, or document. Fail: "historians say,"
"nobody will tell you," or any other uncited assertion.

**Test 2 — The room pushes back (corroboration).** Pass: independently
corroborated by an unconnected second source. Fail: single-source, or actively
complicated/contradicted by a second source. Surviving claims get tagged
`corroborated` or `single-source-uncorroborated` rather than a binary discard —
the tag itself is useful downstream information, not just a pass/fail gate.

**Test 3 — Depth over heat.** Pass: the claim holds once dramatic narration,
music-cue framing, or insinuation is stripped away. Fail: the claim's
persuasive force depends on the packaging, not on what's actually being
asserted.

**Test 4 — Argument not category.** Pass: the claim is about a specific text,
person, or event. Fail: the content's core mechanism villain-codes a whole
category (e.g. "the establishment," "black British people," "Anglo-Saxons")
rather than engaging specific counter-evidence. The accusatory frame never gets
imported into WW content even when the underlying facts happen to survive.

## 3. Overall per-video/per-piece verdict

- **Full pass** — clears for direct programme use, citable as-is.
- **Conditional pass** — usable material exists but needs
  structural-parallel-vs-lineage-claim tagging (per the template established in
  the Pageturners griot-tutor oral archive) before use — i.e. flagging where a
  piece draws a resemblance between two traditions without claiming direct
  historical descent, so WW's own retelling doesn't accidentally assert a
  lineage the source never claimed.
- **Fail** — not usable as content directly, but the piece itself can become a
  Rayd-yo media-literacy case study on the gap between packaging and evidence.
  This is a real destination, not a dead end — several of the strongest worked
  examples below are fails repurposed exactly this way.

## 4. Relationship to WW-SPEC-THESE-ARE-THE-FACTS-001

TATF is this rubric's live/on-air enforcement arm. Where this document governs
pre-production filtering of found/archived material, TATF governs what happens
when a claim is made live, or slips past pre-production, with no "before" to
filter. TATF maps each of the four tests to its own correction flavour (Source
Flag / Corroboration Flag / Substance Flag / Framing Flag) — see
WW-SPEC-THESE-ARE-THE-FACTS-001 §2 for the full mapping. That mapping is
downstream of this document and should be read as depending on it, not
duplicating it.

## 5. Worked examples

Kept as a live, growing set — each one teaches a different failure mode, not
just a single false claim.

### 5.1 — Merit-Ptah (sympathetic/well-intentioned error)

Checked as "the first named woman physician in science" and found to be a
documented case of mistaken identity: a 1938 historian's error conflating the
real Old Kingdom figure Peseshet ("lady overseer of the female physicians,"
though whether she practised medicine herself is uncertain) with a fictional,
back-dated identity. Kept as the example of the rubric catching an error made
in good faith, not just a bad-faith source — Test 1 fails even when nobody
intended to mislead.

### 5.2 — Feynman's blank textbook and cargo cult science (rating without checking)

Two related cases from Feynman's own published writing (*Surely You're Joking,
Mr. Feynman*; the 1974 "Cargo Cult Science" address), safe to treat as solid
since both are primary-source, not secondary retellings:

- On California's 1964 textbook commission, Feynman was the only member who
  actually read the submitted books rather than delegating to local reviewers
  and averaging scores. One publisher's book arrived completely blank inside
  the covers (a missed deadline) and was still rated by commissioners who never
  opened it — scoring slightly higher than the same publisher's two completed
  textbooks. Maps to Tests 1 and 2 together: a rating that looks sourced
  without ever being connected to the thing it claims to evaluate. (The
  specific claim that the blank book scored higher than the real submissions
  should be re-confirmed against the primary account before being quoted as an
  exact figure — the well-established core fact either way is that a blank,
  unopened book was rated at all.)
- His account of Pacific islanders replicating airstrip infrastructure
  (runways, control towers, wooden headphones, a man waving landing signals)
  after wartime supply planes stopped coming, with the actual underlying
  mechanism never present. Maps to Test 3: a claim can have the full shape of
  rigour — confident delivery, apparent authority — while the substance
  underneath was never checked. Feynman's own first principle from the same
  address: "you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to
  fool."

### 5.3 — Viral "diaspora wars" reaction video (Black British vs. Black American identity content)

Clean fail across three tests: Test 1 (pure assertion, no sourcing anywhere),
Test 3 (runs entirely on rhetorical momentum and repetition rather than
surviving with the heat stripped out), and especially Test 4 (villain-codes
"black British people"/"the BBs" as a category rather than engaging any
specific claim or person). Repurposed as a Rayd-yo media-literacy case study
rather than discarded.

### 5.4 — Far-right deportation-debate clip ("millions must go" / pro-Empire debate)

The host's category-based dehumanisation of migrants is an outright Test 4 fail
requiring no further checking. The two debaters' historical claims were
genuinely mixed on a per-claim basis — real, checkable errors sat beside
genuinely solid sourcing in the same breath: one debater's Isandlwana account
invented a "one spared messenger" detail and omitted that Britain won the war
and annexed Zululand afterward (Test 1/2 fail); the other's claim that "the
British government was never the initiator of slavery" is false, since the
Crown directly chartered and governed the Royal African Company from 1660/1672
(Test 1/2 fail) — while Thomas Thistlewood's diary and the $20,000
Japanese-American internment reparations figure, cited by the same speakers,
checked out correctly (Test 1/2 pass). The lesson this example teaches:
confident, fluent delivery carries real and invented claims with identical
conviction — which is precisely why per-claim checking, not overall impression,
has to be the method.

### 5.5 — "The Black Academics Dilemma" monologue

The strongest-passing "hot take" content checked to date. Passes Test 3 and
Test 4 cleanly — a genuine structural argument about academic incentive
structures rather than rhetorical momentum, critiquing specific institutional
dynamics rather than villain-coding a category, correctly invoking real
historical figures (Carter G. Woodson, W.E.B. Du Bois, Anna Julia Cooper) and a
well-documented historical pattern (Black-built economic institutions destroyed
via redlining, urban renewal, highway construction, predatory lending). One
claim flagged for nuance rather than failure: a reference to a UK academic's
Cambridge appointment and its controversy (Jason Arday) is more complicated on
the full record than the monologue's framing suggests — real, independently
reported CV/scholarship discrepancies existed alongside a documented,
racially-motivated coordinated campaign that applied a demonstrable double
standard against him versus a white colleague in an almost identical situation.
Both things are true at once; flattening to either side alone would repeat the
exact error this rubric exists to catch.
