## 1. Purpose

This document specifies how Rayd-yo hosts and presenters are expected to conduct
themselves on air, distinct from what gets vetted before it reaches a script
(that's the source-vetting pipeline's job). This is about live conduct: how a
host questions a guest, how a host handles an unverified claim in the moment,
and how a host is trained to notice — and act on — the difference between a
claim that holds up and one that doesn't.

The founding premise: toughness of tone is not the same thing as accuracy of
content, and a channel can fail in either direction — by being uncritically soft
(letting confident, credentialed claims pass unchallenged) or by being
performatively hard (aggressive, combative delivery with no actual sourcing
underneath it). Rayd-yo is built to avoid both failure modes, not just the
first one.

## 2. The core principle: sourcing before toughness

Adversarial interviewing technique — persistence, refusing to let a non-answer
pass, repeating the question — is a delivery mechanism, never a substitute for
research. It only works because the host already knows the specific fact, quote,
or figure the guest is dodging.

- **What this looks like done right:** the host has pre-loaded sourcing on the
  episode's load-bearing claims — the 2–3 facts the argument actually turns on —
  before recording. When a guest's answer conflicts with that sourcing, the host
  holds the line: repeats the question, cites the specific document or figure,
  doesn't move on until it's actually addressed.
- **What this looks like done wrong (soft failure):** a guest's credentials (a
  doctorate, a title, a public profile) are allowed to substitute for
  verification. The host nods, doesn't follow up, treats confident delivery as
  evidence of accuracy.
- **What this looks like done wrong (hard failure):** the host is aggressive,
  interrupts, performs scepticism as a house style — but hasn't actually checked
  the claim against a primary source, so the hostility isn't attached to
  anything. This is theatre wearing rigor's clothes, and it fails the same way
  soft-balling does: the audience can't actually tell whether the claim was
  true, only that the host was in a bad mood about it.

**Rule:** no on-air toughness without off-air sourcing behind it, specific to
the claim being challenged.

## 3. The silence-as-endorsement principle

An unchallenged claim in an interview format does not read to the audience as
"unverified, decide for yourself." It reads as vetted — because the format
itself looks like scrutiny (a host present, a platform, an apparent editorial
process) even when no actual scrutiny occurred. A disclaimer or a stated "we
offer a range of voices" policy does not undo this. The audience's read is on
the format, not the fine print.

This means silence is never a neutral act on Rayd-yo. A host who lets a claim
pass without challenge has functionally vouched for it, whether or not that was
the intent.

**Case study (negative):** BBC World Service's December 2024 investigation of
*Diary of a CEO* found an average of 14 harmful health claims per episode across
the episodes reviewed — including a guest presenting a ketogenic diet as a
cancer treatment alternative to chemotherapy, and another calling COVID vaccines
"a net negative to society" — with the host rarely challenging any of it. The
production company's defence ("open-minded," "thoroughly researched," "freedom
of expression, not agreement") did not change how the unchallenged claims landed
with an audience of millions. The format's silence did the vouching the
disclaimer tried to disclaim.

## 4. Waffle-detection as a trained procedure, not a personality trait

A guest with real sourcing behind a claim tends to get more precise under
pressure — names, dates, papers, numbers. A guest without it reaches for volume,
repetition, reframing the question, or appeals to authority instead of evidence.
This is observable in real time and doesn't require the host to have
pre-researched every conceivable claim a guest might make.

**Case study (what a well-sourced challenge looks like when it lands):** Alfredo
"Dr. Sebi" Bowman's claimed cures for AIDS and cancer are not merely disputed
opinion — there is a specific, checkable record: a 1993 New York Attorney
General consumer-fraud consent judgment found his therapeutic claims
unsubstantiated and legally barred him from making them. A host who knows that
one fact going in doesn't need to be combative in tone to close the claim down;
they need to ask "where's the trial data, and how does that square with the 1993
judgment against you making exactly this claim" — and hold the question open
until it's actually answered.

**Rule:** hosts are trained to notice the sound of an unanswered question and
keep it open, rather than moving the segment along to avoid dead air or friction.
This is teachable and auditable — closer to cross-examination discipline than to
a debate-club performance style — and does not depend on any individual host's
natural temperament.

## 5. Pre-episode homework requirement

Hosts do not attempt real-time fact-checking live. Before recording, the
production team identifies the 2–3 claims that are actually load-bearing for
that episode's argument or guest's position, and sources them — supporting or
contradicting evidence, with citation — as part of standard pre-production, the
same discipline already required by the source-vetting pipeline for
pre-recorded material.

This pre-loaded sourcing is what the host draws on during Section 2's "hold the
line" moments. Without it, adversarial technique has nothing to push against and
collapses into either soft-balling (nothing to challenge with) or empty
aggression (challenging without basis).

## 6. Interviewing models — what to take from each, and what not to

Rayd-yo does not adopt a single house style wholesale. Distinct techniques serve
distinct purposes:

- **Robin Day / Jeremy Paxman:** pure adversarial persistence — repeating a
  specific question until it's actually answered, refusing to accept a
  non-answer as an answer. Works only because the interviewer already holds the
  fact being dodged.
- **Mehdi Hasan:** persistence combined with visibly pre-loaded sourcing — the
  guest's own past statements, a specific document, a specific number, deployed
  live as the basis for the challenge.
- **John Oliver:** the rigor happens in advance, in the writing room, over an
  extended research period; on-air delivery is long-form and comic rather than
  confrontational. Useful model for recorded segments, not live interview.
- **Caution — Ben Shapiro-style rapid-fire debate delivery:** fast, confident,
  combative — but the technique itself provides no guarantee of underlying
  accuracy, and this style has been criticised for leaning on cherry-picked or
  decontextualised statistics rather than Hasan-style pre-sourced primary
  material. Speed and confidence are not evidence. Do not mistake this style's
  energy for its rigor.

**Rule:** borrow technique, not tone. The tone is downstream of whether the
homework was done, in every model above that actually works.

## 7. Relationship to existing WW specs

- **Test 0 (selection), `WW-SPEC-SOURCE-VETTING-RUBRIC-001.md`:** this
  document's principles apply live, on-air conduct; Test 0 applies to
  pre-production source vetting (does the source material itself present a fair
  range of evidence, not just confirming material). A host can execute this
  document's principles perfectly and still air content that fails Test 0 if the
  underlying pre-production vetting didn't catch a one-sided source. Both layers
  are required.
- **TATF (`WW-SPEC-THESE-ARE-THE-FACTS-001.md`):** a host correctly executing
  Section 4 (waffle-detection) during a live segment is the natural human-side
  trigger for a live Source Flag or Corroboration Flag under the existing TATF
  mechanism. This document's "keep it open" instruction and TATF's
  live-correction flow should be trained together, not as separate skills.

## 8. The reflexivity clause

Being the institution that does the checking does not grant permanent immunity
from becoming the thing being checked. Rayd-yo's own hosts, scripts, and claims
are subject to the same four-test rubric and this same doctrine as any external
source material. "We are the fact-checkers" is itself an unexamined credential
if it is ever treated as self-certifying rather than something re-earned, on
air, claim by claim, the same way everyone else's claims are.

## 9. Open items (not resolved by this document)

- **Ownership:** who is accountable for auditing Rayd-yo's own on-air output
  against Test 0 and this doctrine — a named role under the staff-room model, or
  folded into existing editorial workflow. Not yet decided (mirrors the same
  open ownership question already flagged in the source-vetting pipeline for
  consumer/budget-vetting content).
- **Training format:** whether this doctrine is delivered as a written brief, a
  workshop with recorded practice interviews, or both.
- **Escalation:** what happens when a host fails to close down an unanswered
  claim live — whether this routes into TATF's recorded-track correction after
  the fact, and who reviews episodes for this after broadcast.
