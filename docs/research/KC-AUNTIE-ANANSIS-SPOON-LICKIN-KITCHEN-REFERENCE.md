## COOKIE — Technique & Safety Notes

*What to say while your hands are actually on the food.*

### Legumes

- **Kidney beans (and cannellini, a white kidney variant):** raw beans
  contain phytohaemagglutinin. Slow cookers often don't get hot enough to
  destroy it — soaking alone isn't enough. Always give dried
  kidney/cannellini beans a vigorous boil, not just a soak, before
  slow-cooking or stewing.
- **Lima beans and fava beans:** wild ancestors carry cyanogenic
  compounds (linamarin), bred down over generations in modern varieties
  — but dried beans should still always be cooked thoroughly, not eaten
  raw.
- **Fava beans specifically:** a minority of people (mostly of
  Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, African, or South Asian ancestry) carry
  G6PD deficiency and can have a serious reaction (favism) to fava beans
  — genuine allergy-adjacent caution worth a spoken heads-up on camera,
  not just a footnote.
- **Chickpeas (aquafaba):** the canning liquid whips into stiff peaks
  like egg whites — real technique, not a gimmick, good for vegan
  meringues/mousses.
- **Winged bean:** genuinely edible root-to-tip — leaves like spinach,
  pods like green beans, roots like a starchy tuber. Whole-plant utility,
  good visual demo.

### Nuts

- **Cashews:** never demo cracking a raw cashew shell — it contains a
  urushiol-family oil (same toxic-oil family as poison ivy) and needs
  roasting/professional processing first. Only handle pre-shelled cashews
  on camera.
- **Bitter almonds:** contain cyanogenic compounds — sweet almonds
  (grocery-store standard) are the only safe variety to cook with raw.
- **Brazil nuts:** naturally contain trace natural radioactivity from
  soil-absorbed radium — genuinely harmless at normal eating quantities,
  good "surprising but safe" beat.
- **Hickory "milk":** crushed shell-and-all hickory nuts boiled in water
  make a real, historically-documented creamy liquid, usable in soups —
  worth demoing as a technique, not just a story.
- **Acorns:** inedible raw due to tannins — must be shelled, crushed, and
  repeatedly leached/rinsed in water before use in flour or porridge.
  Real technique, not folklore.

### Raw vs. Cooked Vegetables

- **Kale, broccoli, cauliflower, arugula, radish, watercress (all
  cruciferous):** chopping/chewing activates enzymes (myrosinase) that
  convert glucosinolates into the compounds responsible for their
  peppery/sharp flavour and studied health compounds (e.g. sulforaphane)
  — cutting and letting it rest a few minutes before cooking maximises
  this; heat can reduce the enzyme's activity, so raw preparation
  preserves more of it.
- **Kale specifically:** raw kale is tough — massaging chopped leaves
  with a little acid/dressing for a minute or two physically breaks down
  the fibre and makes it palatable.
- **Spinach:** pair raw spinach with a vitamin-C source (citrus, red
  pepper, strawberries) to help the body absorb its non-heme iron better.
- **Carrots and other carotenoid vegetables:** cooking (and adding a
  little fat) makes beta-carotene more absorbable than eating them raw
  and plain — the "raw is always healthier" framing doesn't hold here.
- **Fennel:** the bulb is not a root — it's swollen leaf-stalk bases. Raw
  is sharper/more liquorice-forward; cooking mellows the anise flavour.
- **Cucumbers:** ~95% water — a genuinely hydrating ingredient, not
  "just" a filler vegetable.
- **Beetroot:** raw beetroot can turn urine/stool pink or red (beeturia)
  — harmless, worth a heads-up so nobody panics.

### Steak & Meat Technique

*From the 20-method deep dive — 16 of 20 confirmed solid; see
`KC-STEAK-TECHNIQUE-REFERENCE.md` for the full per-method rating.*

- Reverse sear: gentle low heat first, then a brief, very hot sear at the
  end — well-established technique.
- Dry-brine overnight, uncovered, on a rack — real, effective.
- Never bring meat to room temperature before cooking — it makes
  negligible difference; confirmed against serious food-science testing.
- 40-minute freezer surface-dry before searing — real trick for a better
  crust.
- Buy thick cuts (1.5–2 inches minimum) for these techniques to work
  properly.
- Two-zone fire (hot side + cool side) for live-fire cooking.
- Dry the surface with a towel immediately before searing — moisture is
  the enemy of a good crust.
- Cast iron needs a genuinely long preheat (6–8 minutes) before the meat
  goes in.
- Continuous fat-basting during the sear, and standing the steak on its
  fat cap to render it — both real.
- Never press, fork, or cut into meat to check doneness — use a
  thermometer.
- Marinades only penetrate a few millimetres into a thick cut — don't
  oversell marinating as a deep-flavour technique.
- Baking soda ("velveting") genuinely tenderises tough cuts.
- Always cut against the grain.

**⚠️ Caveat to build in on camera:** pulling steak at 115°F expecting
15°F of carryover heat is probably too aggressive for a quick sear on a
thin steak (that much carryover is realistic for a big roast with a long
rest, not a 90-second sear) — pull closer to 120°F for medium-rare with
this method.

**⚠️ Caveat:** "olive oil burns" is true for extra virgin (~375°F smoke
point) but not really true for refined/light olive oil (~465°F) — don't
state it as a blanket fact; specify which olive oil.

### General Food-Safety Reminders Worth Repeating On Camera

- Raw leafy greens (romaine etc.) carry real foodborne-illness risk from
  time to time — proper washing, discarding damaged leaves, and following
  recall notices matters more for raw prep than cooked.
- Wild-foraged watercress can carry parasites from contaminated water —
  only use commercially grown watercress for on-camera raw prep.

## ASO — Story Hooks & Cultural History

*What to say while the pot's doing the work.*

### Origins & Etymology (quick, colourful, safe to state as fact)

- **Pinto beans:** "pinto" is Spanish for "painted" — the raw bean is
  streaked reddish-brown before cooking, then fades to plain. And
  "refried beans" is a mistranslation — *frijoles refritos* means
  "well-fried," not "fried twice."
- **Cannellini/cranberry beans (borlotti):** the same bean goes by
  different names depending where you are — *cargamanto* in Colombia,
  *borlotti* in Italy, cranberry bean in the US.
- **Walnut:** the Romans called it "Jupiter's acorn" (*Juglans* = *Jovis
  glans*) — reserved for royalty by ancient Persians.
- **Almond:** its Greek root, *amygdala*, is the same word used for the
  almond-shaped part of the brain that processes emotion.
- **Hazelnut/filbert:** the English name "filbert" comes from St
  Philibert, whose feast day lines up with the harvest.
- **Coconut:** Portuguese and Spanish explorers named it after "coco" —
  a grinning or frightening face — because of the three dark markings on
  the shell.
- **Lentil:** the word "lens" (as in eyeglasses and cameras) is named
  after the lentil's shape, not the other way round.
- **Pecan:** the word comes from an Algonquian term roughly meaning "a
  nut that requires a stone to crack."
- **Winter melon, water chestnut, tomatillo, tamarillo:** good "things
  aren't what their name says" material — a water chestnut isn't a nut
  (it's a corm), a tomatillo isn't a tomato, and the tamarillo was
  renamed from "tree tomato" by New Zealand in 1967 purely for export
  marketing.

### Real Named People Worth Telling Their Story

- **Antoine**, an enslaved horticulturist in 1800s Louisiana, developed
  the grafting technique that turned pecans from unpredictable wild trees
  into the reliable, commercially graftable crop the whole modern pecan
  industry is built on — largely uncredited in his own lifetime. Strong,
  true, under-told story.
- **George Washington Carver** genuinely developed hundreds of practical
  peanut-based products and pushed struggling Southern farmers toward
  crop rotation to restore depleted soil — real history (he did not
  invent peanut butter, a common myth worth correcting rather than
  repeating).
- **William Whitehouse**, a Syrian immigrant, laid the foundation of
  California's commercial pistachio industry in the early 1900s.

### Legends Worth Telling As Legends

*(real stories, correctly framed as unresolved or traditional rather than
settled fact)*

- Pythagoras reportedly forbade his followers from eating fava beans —
  nobody knows why for certain; theories range from spiritual reasons to
  an early, unrecognised brush with favism. Great "even the ancients
  half-understood this" hook — tell it as an open mystery, not a solved
  one.
- The Queen of Sheba is said to have loved pistachios so much she claimed
  an entire harvest for her own court — tell as legend.
- The US Senate has served bean soup daily for well over a century — true
  and confirmed by the Senate's own historian. Why it started is
  genuinely disputed between two rival senators' origin stories — tell
  both versions as "nobody's sure which one's true," not as settled fact.
- Coconut shells really were used to make activated charcoal for
  WWI/WWII gas mask filters, and there really was a public collection
  campaign — but it also involved peach and plum stones, not coconut
  alone, and the specific tonnage claims floating around online don't
  hold up (see the ⚠️ section below for the real figure). Tell the real
  mechanism (coconut charcoal traps gas), skip the invented numbers.
- Moringa leaves are said to have fuelled ancient warriors before battle
  — a widely repeated claim in modern "superfood" marketing, traced back
  to Mauryan-era India rather than anywhere specific in West Africa.
  Frame as "the story goes," not documented history.
- Quinoa was reportedly banned by Spanish colonisers as "food for
  Indians" — this story is everywhere, but rarely traced back to a
  specific primary document. Tell it as widely-reported popular history,
  not airtight fact.

### Diaspora-Relevant Threads

*(natural fit for Aso's storytelling half)*

- **Bambara groundnut** is named after the Bambara people of present-day
  Mali — one of Africa's most important, most underappreciated food
  crops, ranking behind only peanuts and cowpeas in many regions.
- **Black-eyed peas** originated in West Africa, crossed the Atlantic via
  the slave trade, and became rooted in the American South — including
  the real New Year's Day "luck" tradition (Hoppin' John).
- **Cassava** made a genuine round trip: Portuguese traders carried it
  from Brazil to West/Central Africa in the 1500s, so many enslaved
  Africans crossing the Middle Passage already knew the plant — one of
  very few points of real culinary continuity across that crossing.
- **Yellow yam** is tied to the Jamaican Maroons, who hid yam patches
  deep in the forest to survive outside British control — and Usain Bolt
  has repeatedly credited yellow yam as part of his childhood diet.
- **Adzuki beans:** a 2025 genetic study (published in *Science*)
  confirmed they were actually domesticated in Japan during the Jōmon
  period, overturning the older belief that they were introduced from
  mainland Asia — a nice "the science just settled a centuries-old
  question" beat.

### Fun/Surprising Science

*(safe one-liners for Aso between stirs)*

- Green and red bell peppers are the same fruit — red is just left on
  the plant longer to ripen and sweeten.
- The WWII "carrots give pilots night vision" story was genuine British
  wartime propaganda — used to cover for the real secret, radar, while
  also encouraging people to eat a wartime-available vegetable.
- Cauliflower, romanesco, purple cauliflower, and orange cauliflower are
  all the same species, bred for different pigments.
- A beech tree doesn't produce nuts evenly every year — it "masts,"
  producing almost nothing most years and then flooding the forest with
  nuts every several years, so predators can't eat them all.
- Pine nuts can cause "pine mouth" — a real, harmless, but genuinely
  bizarre metallic taste that can linger for days after eating certain
  species.

## ⚠️ Handle With Care / Do Not State As Flat Fact

*Pulled from the survival-crops archive (the one video in this batch with
the most inflated numbers, per the original vetting — the archive itself
is not present in this repo, see the placement note above) and a couple
of others. These are either invented figures or under-sourced legends —
did not pass clean vetting and should be framed with a caveat or left out
entirely.*

- **"Taro has been cultivated for 28,000 years"** — false; real figure is
  roughly 9,000–10,000 years. Don't repeat the 28,000 number at all.
- **"400 tons of coconut shells collected from home kitchens every day"**
  for WWI gas masks — invented/unsupported figure. The real campaign
  mixed peach stones, plum stones, and various nut shells (coconut
  included but not exclusive) at roughly 750 tons/day of combined
  material.
- **Chickpea "domesticated nearly 12,000 years ago"** — a stretch;
  mainstream figure is closer to 10,000 years. Fine to say "one of the
  oldest domesticated crops," less fine to state the specific 12,000
  figure.
- **Civil War soldiers "ignoring black-eyed pea fields as just animal
  feed"** — repeated Southern-food folklore, not a documented historical
  incident. The broader point (black-eyed peas sustained families through
  shortages) is solid; this specific anecdote isn't.
- **"Violet leaves have more vitamin C than an orange"** — plausible
  (parsley, kale, and Brussels sprouts all genuinely beat oranges gram
  for gram) but not independently confirmed for violets specifically.
  Fine to say "reportedly" rather than stating it flatly.
