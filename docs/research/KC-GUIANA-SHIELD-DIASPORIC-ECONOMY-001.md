# LOCKED — Verified, Sourced Facts

Everything in this section was independently checked on 28 Aug 2026 (Claude Code) unless
a line says otherwise. Where a figure in the original draft was stale or wrong, the
correction is shown inline in **bold** with a date. Web sources are listed at the foot
of this file.

## The boom itself

- Guyana's economy grew **43.6% in 2024** — confirmed — its fifth consecutive year of
  double-digit growth. Oil output rose to an average of **616,000 bpd** (from ~391,000
  bpd in 2023) as the ExxonMobil-led consortium completed an offshore upgrade. Oil
  sector +57.7%, non-oil sector +13.1% in 2024. Confirmed as one of Latin America's
  largest crude exporters (the "fifth-largest" ranking is widely reported;
  ordering shifts with Venezuelan output and is not worth stating to the decimal).

- **Correction (28 Aug 2026):** the draft said "2025 growth forecasts revised upward to
  15.2%." That was an early-2025 forecast figure and is now stale — **2025 real GDP
  growth came in well above it** (IMF and Guyanese government reporting through mid-2026
  put it around 19%+). Oil production continued climbing past 640,000 bpd. Treat "15.2%"
  as a superseded forecast, not the outturn.

- Recoverable reserves in the Stabroek Block exceed **11 billion oil-equivalent
  barrels** and are still being expanded through ongoing exploration — confirmed.

- GDP per capita rose steeply from the late 2010s to over **$30,000 (2024 nominal,
  ~$33,000 on some series)**, with 2028 projections around **$38,000–39,000**.
  **Precision note (28 Aug 2026):** the draft's specific "$4,265 (2018)" and "$12,000
  (2022)" figures could not be tied to a single source — the numbers vary materially by
  measure (nominal GDP per capita vs. GNI per capita vs. PPP; World Bank put 2022 *GNI*
  per capita at $15,050, the threshold that triggered the high-income reclassification).
  The **direction and scale of the change are not in dispute**; the exact start-point
  figures should be stated with their measure attached or not stated.

- Real GDP growth has averaged roughly 40%+/year since 2022 — the fastest in the world
  over that window. By end-2024: large current account surplus (~24% of GDP), gross
  international reserves past $1 billion, and the **Natural Resource Fund holding ~$3.1
  billion** (it accumulated over $1.1 billion in 2024 alone) — confirmed against IMF
  Article IV material.

- Guyana became the **first-ever Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative
  participant to graduate all the way to high-income status** — confirmed. Guyana took
  HIPC relief in 1997 and enhanced-HIPC relief reaching completion point in 2003; the
  World Bank reclassified it as high-income in July 2023 on 2022 GNI. The IMF projects
  continued growth **averaging ~14%/year over the next five years** — confirmed
  (Article IV, 2025).

- Non-oil sector growth: **confirmed ~13% in 2024.** The draft's "13.8–13.9% in 2025"
  is plausible and in line with IMF commentary on continued strong non-oil growth, but
  the specific 2025 decimal was not independently pinned in this pass — treat as
  approximate.

## Institutional position

- Georgetown hosts the **CARICOM Secretariat** itself — confirmed, headquartered in
  Guyana since CARICOM's founding in 1973. Guyana holds the institutional seat of
  Caribbean integration, it isn't petitioning for a role in it.

- **4 July 2023** — on CARICOM's 50th anniversary, Heads of State agreed to extend
  **freedom of movement to all CARICOM nationals** (to live, work and study in any
  member state without restriction). Confirmed. IOM analysis names Guyana's oil and gas
  industry as **"one important economic game changer — with potential large social
  implications"** behind the timing (quote confirmed verbatim). Guyana's own policy
  commentary frames this candidly as a "brain drain and brain gain" balancing act.

## Suriname's parallel trajectory

- **Correction (28 Aug 2026) — this is the largest factual error in the original
  draft.** The draft said "TotalEnergies alone has committed $1.5 billion" with "first
  oil production targeted for 2028." The current, correct picture:
  - The offshore find is in **Block 58**; the development project is named **GranMorgu**
    (Sapakara South and Krabdagu fields, ~180 km offshore).
  - **Final Investment Decision taken October 2024** by TotalEnergies (40%), **APA
    Corporation (40%)** and **Staatsolie (20%)** — APA, not just TotalEnergies, is a
    co-equal operator-partner; the draft omits APA entirely.
  - **Total project investment is ~$10.5 billion**, not $1.5 billion. (The "$1.5bn"
    figure appears to be a stale or partial pre-FID number.)
  - FPSO capacity **up to 220,000 bpd**; **first oil 2028** (this part of the draft is
    right); ~6,000 jobs projected in Suriname.
- The draft's structural point still holds: **Guyana is several years ahead** and has
  already built the sovereign wealth fund architecture, IMF relationship, CARICOM
  leadership position and diplomatic profile (recent UN Security Council term, CARICOM
  chair) that Suriname will need to match or risk becoming the smaller, less
  consequential of the two oil Guianas by default.

## French Guiana's structural bind

- French Guiana is **not a sovereign state** — it is a French overseas department,
  legally part of France and the EU. Combined three-Guianas population is only ~1.7
  million. Confirmed and uncontroversial.

- Academic framing of the three Guianas as a "geopolitical labyrinth" / an interface
  between Caribbean, South American and European spheres rather than a coherent bloc —
  this is a characterisation from the literature, retained as the draft's framing rather
  than a hard fact.

- The **Oyapock River Bridge** (structurally completed 2011, opened to traffic 2017
  after prolonged border-post and regulatory delays) created the first fixed land
  crossing between French Guiana and the rest of the world, connecting to **Brazil**
  (Amapá). Confirmed. The Guyana–Suriname link remains a **ferry crossing at the Maroni
  River (Albina–South Drain / near Albina)**; EU regional development funding has
  targeted upgrading it.

- Connectivity in the region is being built primarily as an **EU external-border and
  Amazon-conservation project** (Interreg Amazonia / Interreg Caraïbes programmes tying
  French Guiana to Suriname and the Brazilian states of Amapá and Amazonas) — Brussels'
  money and priorities, not a Guyana- or Suriname-led integration push. Retained as the
  draft's framing; the Interreg programmes are real.

- **The inversion risk:** if Guyana's per-capita income keeps climbing toward the
  ~$38,000 (2028 projected) range while French Guiana's stays well below mainland French
  averages and dependent on Paris's transfers, French Guiana becomes the visibly poorer,
  dependency-model neighbour beside two increasingly wealthy, self-directed sovereign
  states — a sharpened version of the "overlooked by Paris" grievance that drove the
  2017 French Guiana general strike and protests. This is analysis, not a datable
  event — but the 2017 protests, the underlying income gap, and the transfer dependence
  are all real.

## France entering as a new player

- **France opened its first resident embassy in Georgetown around 1 September 2025** —
  the first EU country with that level of diplomatic representation in Guyana, a major
  upgrade from a relationship previously mediated almost entirely through French Guiana.
  Confirmed.

- Cooperation agenda (confirmed): a **Joint Working Group** on defence, climate, food
  security, infrastructure, technology and tourism; explicit commitment to "the security
  and stability of the Guiana Shield." Concrete steps already on the record: a
  **July 2024 security and military cooperation agreement** signed by the Guyana Defence
  Force Chief of Staff and his French counterpart; Guyanese troops training with French
  forces in French Guiana in the **Fer de Lance 2025** exercise (April 2025); Guyana's
  acquisition of French maritime patrol assets.

- This lands directly on the **Essequibo dispute** — France is positioning itself as a
  security partner helping Guyana patrol contested waters, a materially higher-stakes
  form of involvement than trade. Trade itself is a rounding error by comparison: total
  France–Guyana trade was on the order of **€20 million (2018 figure, ~€19.7m as cited
  in the draft — not re-verified this pass, but immaterial to the point)**, against an
  economy now growing ~15–19%/year on oil. The timing tracks the oil boom and the
  Venezuela dispute closely.

## Essequibo / Venezuela background

- Venezuela has never accepted the **1899 arbitral award** confirming the Essequibo
  region (about two-thirds of Guyana's territory) as Guyanese, and has pursued the claim
  more aggressively since the Stabroek discovery, including a **December 2023 referendum**
  approving annexation. Guyana has no credible independent military capacity to resist
  Venezuela and would depend on international backing if this escalated. Confirmed and
  uncontroversial.

- Independent country-risk assessment flags, alongside the boom: a low-skilled local
  workforce and mass emigration of skilled workers; inadequate transport, electricity,
  education and health infrastructure; inefficient public spending and corruption; high
  crime linked to drug trafficking. The Essequibo dispute is listed as a **live
  structural risk factor for Guyana's economic outlook itself** — investors price
  territorial risk directly into the trajectory.

- **Corruption ranking — correction (28 Aug 2026):** the draft said "87th of 180 on
  Transparency International's CPI." That was the **2023** rank. On the **2024 CPI,
  Guyana scored 39 and ranked 92 of 180** — a decline from 2023 (rank 87) and 2022
  (rank 85). Transparency International's 2024 country note states that **"state capture
  by political and economic elites"** in Guyana "fosters misappropriation of resources,
  illicit enrichment, and an environmental crime." This *strengthens* the draft's own
  governance-risk argument rather than weakening it — but the number and the direction
  both need updating.

- The Natural Resource Fund legislation was passed on a one-seat parliamentary majority
  without opposition or broad civil-society consultation; local watchdogs warned of
  "loopholes that could give way to massive corruption, borrowing frenzies, and spending
  sprees." The IMF is explicitly urging Guyana to close its fiscal deficit (by around
  2031 in the 2025 Article IV framing) and to manage spending against the conservatively
  projected lifespan of oil reserves. Retained; consistent with the 2025 Article IV
  material.

   **Why it's load-bearing for this entry:** it determines whether Essequibo annexation

   spilling across the border — becomes the larger regional threat instead.

   negotiated sense — it followed direct US military action. Describe it as such.

   prices, Venezuelan domestic politics). A snapshot, not a resolved state.

4. **Natural Resource Fund governance outcome.** Whether Guyana's institutions can

5. **Whether CARICOM strengthens or fractures.** Guyana's boom is driving landmark

# Category 6 sub-pass — what the boom narrative is flattening

*Added 28 Aug 2026 (Claude Code) as the first pass on Flagged item 7. Sourced; see
foot of file. This is comparative/historiographical background, not member-facing
lesson content.*

The "world's fastest-growing economy" headline compresses a much longer and more
painful story into a single upward line. Two things it erases:

## 1. Guyana was a commodity economy that hollowed out — twice over

- **The colonial base.** Sugar was the backbone of the economy from the 17th century
  (the industry dates to 1658 under the Dutch West India Company in Essequibo). After
  the abolition of slavery in 1838, the colonial administration brought roughly **240,000
  indentured labourers from India between 1838 and 1917** to keep the plantations
  running — the origin of Guyana's Indo-Guyanese majority. Rice emerged as a secondary
  smallholder export crop, largely developed by those workers and their descendants.
  **Bauxite mining began in 1917.** For decades after independence (1966), Guyana stayed
  locked into a classic colonial dependency: sugar, bauxite, rice, gold — commodity
  exports exposed to every swing in world prices, with infrastructure confined almost
  entirely to the coastal strip.

- **The nationalisation era and the collapse.** Through the 1970s the Forbes Burnham
  government nationalised the US and Canadian bauxite holdings and, in 1976, the vast
  Booker McConnell holdings (sugar plus much of the country's light manufacturing and
  commerce). Combined with authoritarian "cooperative socialism," state economic
  planning and a foreign-exchange crisis, the result by the late 1970s and through the
  1980s was **endemic shortages, a stagnating or shrinking GDP per capita, and a
  functionally bankrupt state** — the conditions that made Guyana a HIPC debt-relief
  case in the first place. The "first HIPC to reach high-income" headline only makes
  sense against that floor: Guyana isn't a poor country that got lucky, it's a country
  that was deliberately impoverished by policy and then, decades later, sat on top of an
  oil field.

## 2. Guyana has been a *net exporter of people* for two generations

- **The scale.** By the standard estimates, **around 55% of Guyanese citizens live
  abroad**, and **over 80% of Guyanese with a university education have emigrated** —
  one of the highest skilled-emigration rates in the world. The principal destinations
  are the **UK, US, Canada, and Trinidad & Tobago**. There are long-established
  Guyanese populations in London (Windrush-era and after) and very large ones in New
  York and Toronto.

- **It was continuous, not a single wave.** Burnham launched a formal *remigration*
  scheme as early as **1967** — one year after independence — to try to reverse brain
  drain that was already underway. Outflows intensified through the 1970s and 1980s;
  unofficial estimates put departures in the late 1980s at **10,000–30,000 a year** from
  a population then well under a million. Emigration in that period was also ethnically
  patterned: many of those leaving were middle-class professionals, disproportionately
  Indo-Guyanese, responding to a public sector that favoured Afro-Guyanese employment
  under the PNC.

- **Why this matters for the diaspora angle.** The Windrush-generation Guyanese-British
  family watching the boom from London is not watching a stranger's country strike it
  rich. They are, very often, watching the specific place their parents or grandparents
  *left because it was failing* — and that departure was frequently framed at the time
  as permanent, even shameful. The boom inverts a fifty-year emotional settlement:
  "there was nothing to go back to" is abruptly no longer obviously true. That is the
  original, testimony-shaped material this entry exists to reach — and it is exactly the
  thing a GDP chart cannot show.

- **The bitter present-day irony to hold in view:** independent risk assessments of the
  boom list, in the same breath as the growth figures, "a low-skilled local workforce
  and mass emigration of skilled workers" as a *constraint on Guyana's ability to
  capture its own oil wealth*. The country spent fifty years exporting the engineers,
  doctors and teachers it now needs — and the 2023 CARICOM free-movement decision, which
  Guyana's own commentators frame as a "brain drain / brain gain" gamble, is partly an
  attempt to import replacements from the rest of the Caribbean.

**Still open in this sub-pass:** the pre-oil political history is contested terrain in
Guyana itself (the Burnham vs. Jagan / PNC vs. PPP argument is live and ethnically
charged to this day) — any WW treatment has to present the nationalisation-era collapse
without adopting one party's account of it wholesale. Test 4 of the vetting rubric
(argument, not category) applies here as much as it does to the Venezuela material.

# Windrush-generation testimony angle — scoping note (not yet actioned)

*Added 28 Aug 2026 (Claude Code) against Flagged item 6. This scopes the work; it does
not do it. No testimony has been gathered and none should be represented as gathered.*

**Why it's the priority.** Everything else in this entry is assembled from public IMF,
World Bank, IOM, TotalEnergies and news sources — genuinely useful synthesis, but not
something only WW could produce. The Windrush-generation testimony is the part that is
*only* reachable through WW's actual community relationships and Judith's editorial
work. It is what turns a current-affairs briefing into a research-vessel finding.

**What it would consist of.** First-person accounts from Guyanese-British families —
ideally spanning the generation that emigrated (1950s–1980s), the generation born in
Britain, and any who have since remigrated or are considering it — on a narrow set of
questions:
- What was said, at the time of leaving, about whether Guyana had a future?
- How was Guyana talked about at home while the children were growing up in Britain?
- What has the oil boom changed about that conversation — remittances, property,
  retirement plans, how the younger generation identifies?
- Is there resentment, vindication, grief, suspicion, or some mixture — and does it
  divide along the same Indo-/Afro-Guyanese lines that shaped the original emigration?

**Where it lives if built.** `src/pages/programmes/roots/RootsArchive.tsx` — as a
Judith-led (`leadBy` / `judith-leads`) section, evidence-graded `traditional` or
`research` rather than `documented`, with the podcast slot (`PodcastEpisode`) as the
natural delivery format. It cross-links back to this entry for the economic context.

**What must not happen.** No composite or illustrative "testimony" written to stand in
for the real thing while it's being gathered — that is exactly the invented-content
failure mode this project's discipline exists to prevent. Until real accounts exist,
this section stays a scoping note.

**Dependency.** This needs a decision from Judith / CJ on whether it's in scope for the
Roots podcast and whether the community contacts exist to gather it ethically (consent,
attribution, the safeguarding considerations around recorded personal testimony). Not a
Claude Code task.

# Track 1 vetting notes (per WW's four-test rubric, applied to this subject)

*(The four-test rubric is now formalised in
`docs/research/WW-SPEC-SOURCE-VETTING-RUBRIC-001.md`. This section predates

  for Essequibo and post-Maduro material, where competing government statements are the

  framing). This is where the discipline matters most in this entry.
- **Test 4 (argument, not category).** Anything touching US immigration/foreign policy,
  French colonial history, or Guyana's internal ethnic politics stays on specific,
  documented policies and their effects — no wholesale villain-coding of "America,"
  "France," or either Guyanese party as a category.
