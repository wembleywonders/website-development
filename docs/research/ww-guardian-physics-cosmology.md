## Pearleen / Herald — Electromagnetism

**Domain status:** locked here — "Pearleen/Herald = Electromagnetism
(she's the street's literal signal-propagation channel)."

### The real mechanism: two things that turned out to be one thing

For most of scientific history, electricity and magnetism were treated as
completely unrelated. Sparks, batteries, and lightning sat in one
category. Lodestones and compass needles sat in another. Nobody had a
reason to think they were connected — until 1820, when a Danish lecturer
named Ørsted noticed something odd during a demonstration: a compass
needle sitting near a wire twitched the moment current started flowing
through it. There was no magnet anywhere near it. The current itself was
doing something a magnet does. That single accidental twitch was the
first evidence that electricity and magnetism weren't two separate
stories at all.

The person who chased that connection hardest had no business being a
physicist by the standards of the day. Michael Faraday grew up poor, left
school early, and taught himself science while working as a bookbinder's
apprentice — reading the very books he was meant to be sealing shut. He
talked his way into a laboratory job, and through sheer persistence
became one of the most gifted experimentalists of his century. What he
never had was formal mathematics — no calculus, no equations. What he had
instead was an extraordinary physical intuition. In 1831 he ran the
mirror-image experiment to Ørsted's: if electricity could produce
magnetism, could magnetism produce electricity? He found that a
stationary magnet near a coil of wire did nothing at all — but the
instant he moved the magnet, current flowed. Stop moving it, the current
stopped. It wasn't the magnet itself that mattered. It was change. A
shifting magnetic field could push electricity into being. That single
discovery is the working principle behind every power station on Earth,
however the turbine gets turned.

Faraday went further still, and this is the part that got him mocked
rather than praised. To explain how a magnet could act on something it
never touched, he pictured invisible lines filling the space around it —
a field. The professional mathematicians of his day considered this
charming nonsense: real physics was forces between particles, not a
self-taught tradesman's poetic diagram. Faraday couldn't defend the idea
with equations. He simply knew, from years of careful hands-on work, that
it was real.

What the field needed was someone who could translate intuition into
mathematics — and that translator was James Clerk Maxwell. Where Faraday
sensed the field, Maxwell could write it down. He took Faraday's
"childish" lines of force completely seriously and formalised them into
four equations describing everything then known about electricity and
magnetism: how charges create electric fields; the strange fact that no
one has ever found an isolated magnetic pole, only ever paired
north-and-south; Faraday's discovery that a changing magnetic field
creates an electric one; and Ørsted's discovery in reverse, that a
current creates a magnetic field.

Then Maxwell noticed his own fourth equation had a hole in it. Picture a
capacitor — two metal plates with a gap between them. Current flows into
one plate and out of the other, but never actually crosses the gap
itself; the gap only ever holds a growing electric field. By the existing
equation, the magnetic field around the wire should simply vanish at that
gap, which made no physical sense. Maxwell had no experiment and no data
pointing him toward a fix — only a conviction that the mathematics of the
universe had to hang together. So he proposed something with essentially
no evidence behind it: that a changing electric field, on its own, could
create a magnetic field, exactly as a current does. He called it the
displacement current. It was a guess made almost entirely on faith in
consistency.

That guess is what makes the whole system work. Once it's in place,
Faraday's law and Maxwell's new term face each other directly: a changing
magnetic field creates an electric field, and a changing electric field
creates a magnetic field. Shake a single charged particle, and the two
fields begin handing energy back and forth — electric, magnetic,
electric, magnetic — regenerating each other outward through empty space,
needing no medium to travel through at all. That handoff is a wave. And
when Maxwell calculated its speed from two completely unrelated
laboratory constants — one measured from the pull between charges, one
from the pull between magnets — the number that fell out was the
already-known speed of light. Light itself was revealed to be nothing
more than electricity and magnetism passing a signal between each other,
endlessly, at a fixed and universal speed.

The proof came after Maxwell's own lifetime. He died in 1879, before the
wider scientific world accepted what his equations predicted. Eight years
later, Heinrich Hertz built a simple spark-and-loop apparatus and
detected an invisible wave crossing his lab exactly as Maxwell's
mathematics said it should — inventing, without quite meaning to, the
physical basis of every radio signal, phone call, and wireless connection
since.

### Origin-fit for Pearleen/Herald

Pearleen's assignment as "the street's literal signal-propagation
channel" lines up with this mechanism almost exactly. A herald's function
is carrying a message across distance without needing to physically touch
every point along the way — which is precisely what an electromagnetic
wave does: it doesn't travel through something, it regenerates itself
outward, each field handing the signal to the next. The same wave that
carries a radio broadcast, a Wi-Fi signal, and visible light is one
phenomenon stretched or compressed to different sizes — a single
underlying signal taking whatever form the moment calls for, which is a
natural resonance for a character whose role is carrying the same
essential message through however many different channels the street
actually uses.

There's a second, quieter fit worth naming: Faraday sensed the field
before anyone could prove it existed, and needed Maxwell to give it a
form the wider world would accept. A herald carrying real information
that hasn't yet been formally recognised — trusted instinct running ahead
of official confirmation — sits comfortably alongside that history, if
it's ever useful as a character beat rather than just a domain
assignment.

### Physical anchor point

The clearest anchor is the electromagnetic spectrum itself: the same
wave, at different scales, is radio, microwave, visible light,
ultraviolet, and gamma radiation — a single signal broadcasting across a
spectrum far wider than any one receiver can pick up at once, with
different listeners along the street only ever catching the slice tuned
to them.

### Clarke's Law tie-in

This is a strong second proof case for the connecting thesis already
logged here alongside lightning/Zeus/Illapa: an invisible force acting
across distance, dismissed as unprovable folk intuition (Faraday's
mocked "lines of force"), later confirmed as a real, named mechanism
(Maxwell's field equations) — without the earlier intuition becoming
false. The god doesn't get retired. The mechanism just gets a name.

### The real-world case

Quinine — the actual cure for malaria — is what physically enabled
European powers to move off the African coastline and into the interior
from the 1870s onward; before mass production made it affordable, malaria
itself had functioned as a barrier keeping colonising forces out. The
same medicine that could have protected people became the tool that made
their conquest possible, extraction riding in on the back of the one
thing that could have been reciprocal instead.

That pattern repeats as policy eight decades later. In 1955, the World
Health Assembly convened 150 delegates in Mexico City to launch a Global
Malaria Eradication Programme — the first attempt in history to eliminate
a disease entirely, backed by real funding, real science, and DDT. The
plan excluded sub-Saharan Africa by name, over the objections of the
small number of African delegates present, on a vote where wealthier
nations simply outnumbered poorer ones. Hundreds of thousands of deaths
followed in the years directly after, disproportionately children. A
second, later instance of the same withdrawal: pilot eradication
programmes that were working in the early 1960s, driving case counts
toward zero in places like Zanzibar, lost their funding in 1963 when a
single US congressman gutted the foreign aid budget that would have
renewed them — and the continent went without sustained eradication
effort for roughly the next forty years.

This is the antagonist chemistry mechanic as a documented historical
decision rather than a metaphor: a system that took a place in the cycle
(quinine, funding, institutional access) and withdrew before completing
the reciprocal exchange, with the damage compounding across decades
rather than resolving. It sits comfortably alongside the accumulation
parallel already locked in the mechanic — the poison here wasn't a single
act, it was withdrawal sustained over time.
