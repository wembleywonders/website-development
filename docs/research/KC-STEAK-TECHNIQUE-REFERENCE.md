## ✅ Solid — established food science (16 of 20)

- **Reverse sear** — gentle heat (225-250°F) until near target, then a brief, violent
  sear at the end. Genuinely sound: produces an even edge-to-edge doneness rather than
  the grey overcooked band a direct high-heat sear creates.
- **Dry-brining overnight, uncovered, on a rack** — salt draws moisture out, dissolves
  into it, then gets reabsorbed, seasoning the interior; the fridge's dry air
  simultaneously dries the surface for better browning. Real, well-documented technique.
- **Never bringing meat to room temperature first** — an hour on the counter barely
  changes the internal temperature of a thick steak; this specifically matches Kenji
  López-Alt/Serious Eats' published testing.
- **The 40-minute freezer trick** — freezer air is drier than fridge air; 30-45 minutes
  dries the surface fast without freezing the centre. Sound.
- **Thick cuts only (1.5–2in minimum)** — genuinely necessary for the technique; a thin
  steak can't hold a gentle interior and an aggressive crust at once.
- **Two-zone fire** — coals/burner on one side only, meat starts on the cool side.
  Standard, correct grilling practice.
- **Wood for smoke during the slow phase** — a real technique home cooks have access to
  that most professional broilers structurally can't replicate.
- **Drying the surface with a towel right before searing** — water on the surface has to
  boil off (pinning it near 212°F) before real browning (300°F+) can start. Correct
  chemistry.
- **Cast iron, long preheat (6-8 min)** — a thick pan holds heat through contact with
  cold meat; a thin pan's surface temperature collapses on contact. Sound reasoning.
- **Continuous basting during the sear** — cooks the top surface with hot fat while the
  bottom cooks against the metal; genuinely speeds and evens browning.
- **Standing the steak on its fat cap to render it** — real, and genuinely skipped by
  most home cooks; the fat cap won't render properly cooked flat-side only.
- **Never pressing, forking, or cutting to check** — pressing squeezes juice onto the pan
  (cooling it and steaming the meat); forking creates juice-loss channels; cutting to
  check releases heat and juice you can't put back. All correct.
- **Resting mid-process, not at the end** — specific to this method: resting after the
  gentle-heat phase (while the pan heats), not after the final sear, since the slow cook
  already did the interior-evening work a traditional rest is for.
- **Marinades don't penetrate thick cuts** — genuinely only a few millimetres deep after
  a full night; real limitation, correctly stated.
- **Baking soda/velveting for tough cuts** — raises surface pH, loosens protein bonding,
  produces a more tender texture. This is the real mechanism behind Chinese "velveting"
  technique, correctly explained.
- **Cutting against the grain** — genuinely the single biggest factor in perceived
  toughness for cheaper cuts. Correct.

## ⚠️ One number likely overstated

Pulling at 115°F expecting a full 15° of carryover from a 90-second sear. That much
carryover is realistic for a large roast with more thermal mass and a long rest — not
typical for a thin steak off a brief sear. The underlying principle (pull below target,
let residual heat finish the job) is sound; the specific 15° figure is probably too high
for this exact method. Worth pulling closer to 120°F if aiming for medium-rare using this
technique.

## ⚠️ Two claims stated with more certainty than the evidence supports

- **"Pepper burns bitter before the sear finishes browning"** — a commonly repeated
  claim, but genuinely debated in food-science circles; pepper's scorch point may be
  closer to typical searing temperatures than claimed. Reasonable practice, not settled
  science.
- **Koji-rubbed steak (2 days) described as "remarkably close" to 45-day dry-aging** —
  the technique is real and legitimately used for enzymatic tenderising and flavour
  development, but the magnitude of the comparison to true dry-aging is likely oversold.

## Minor imprecision

**"Olive oil burns" stated as a flat fact** — true for extra virgin olive oil (~375°F
smoke point), less true for refined/light olive oil (~465°F, close to typical searing
temperatures). Conflates two different products rather than being simply wrong.
