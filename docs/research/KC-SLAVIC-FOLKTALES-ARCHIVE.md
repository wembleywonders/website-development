### Confirmed accurate — Afanasyev's editorial history

Alexander Afanasyev, a 19th-century Russian folklorist, published his collection
*Narodnye Russkie Skazki* beginning in 1855, running to eight volumes by the
mid-1860s and totalling several hundred/over 600 tales — matches the source
material's framing of him as a Russian counterpart to the Brothers Grimm.

The comparative claim that his collection saw less systematic editorial softening
than Grimm's seven revised editions is a reasonable, well-supported
characterisation, though it's an interpretive comparison rather than a strict
absolute: Afanasyev did make some editorial choices, and separately compiled a
distinct, deliberately unpublished bawdier collection. **Flagged as
interpretive-but-solid if reused** — the same caveat weight as the Wilhelm-Grimm
"visible unease" flag the source material compares it to (that specific Grimm
archive isn't real in this repo — see cross-links above — but the flagging
convention it describes is worth carrying forward here regardless).

### Confirmed accurate — the seven tale retellings

All seven hold up against standard published versions/translations (e.g.
Guterman's *Pantheon Russian Fairy Tales*):

1. **Vasilisa the Beautiful (and Baba Yaga)** — the doll, the three horsemen
   (white/red/black), Baba Yaga's "I smell a Russian soul" formula, the
   fire-skull ending.
2. **The Firebird (Ivan Tsarevich and the Grey Wolf)** — the golden apples, the
   Grey Wolf's shapeshifting aid, the raven/waters-of-death-and-life resurrection.
3. **Koschei the Deathless (Maria Morevna)** — the nested-object hidden death
   (needle/egg/duck/hare/chest/oak/island) and the Maria Morevna frame story.
4. **The Frog Princess (Vasilisa the Wise)** — the skin-burning tragedy and the
   spared-animals repayment at the climax.
5. **Morozko (Father Frost)** — the warmth-for-courtesy test, and the
   well-documented Soviet-era reshaping into Ded Moroz (with Snegurochka as
   companion).
6. **Pushkin's *The Tale of the Dead Princess and the Seven Bogatyrs*** — correct
   title, correct plot beats (1833 verse tale): the magic mirror, Chernavka's
   mercy, the seven bogatyrs, the poisoned apple, Prince Yelisei's sun/moon/wind
   quest.
7. **The Soldier and Death** — the general shape holds as an attested
   Russian/Slavic folk tale type.

### Interpretive framing, not a hard-checkable fact

The political-isolation argument (a Cold War-era translation/circulation gap
limiting Western familiarity with Slavic folklore relative to German/French
tales) is presented as interpretive framing rather than a single hard-checkable
fact. It's a reasonable and widely-echoed explanation, not a fabricated one — but
it should be carried forward as interpretation, not restated as settled history.
