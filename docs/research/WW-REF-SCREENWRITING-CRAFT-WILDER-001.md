## Purpose

Pageturners has a composition tutor (Balla Fasséké — oral epic/poem craft)
and Kaywana's Court has a performance guide (Demodocus — delivery).
Neither covers dramatic/screenwriting craft specifically. This doc is a
working checklist to fill that gap for Easy Street's own scripts — not a
new curator character yet, just the craft rules themselves, applied
directly against structure that's already locked.

If this proves useful in practice, it's the natural source material for a
proper Easy Street screenwriting-craft tutor slot later (Wilder himself, or
his decades-long writing partner I.A.L. Diamond, would need a full Track
1/2 pass first — see `WW-SPEC-SOURCE-VETTING-RUBRIC-001.md` — before either
became a locked curator character).

## The Ten, Applied

### 1. The audience is fickle

No structural hook yet, but a working assumption: don't assume loyalty
from one episode to the next. Every Streetbeats episode needs its own
reason to keep watching, not just accumulated goodwill from the season
arc.

### 2. Grab 'em by the throat and never let go

Applies to: every episode cold open, but especially the Coco Bredd
sketches. The whack-a-mole structure (Church → Council → Parliament → the
King → FIFA → President) only works if each pop-up lands its hook in the
first beat — the joke is who swings and why, and that only reads if the
audience is already gripped when the swing comes. Worth an explicit note
in the Coco Bredd writers' brief: each institution-beat needs its own
throat-grab, not a shared one carried over from the last sketch.

### 3. Develop a clean line of action for your leading character

Applies to: the Tier 3 "forgotten 60%" ensemble (Chuck, Yaw, Afua/Lady
Cynthia, Kumi, Desmond Senior, Budgie). Six centres of drama risk six
blurred lines. Cross-check each one has a single legible want driving
their season, distinct from the economic mobility ladder itself (the
ladder is the shared backdrop, not a substitute for individual want).

### 4. Know where you're going

Applies to: the Walking Policy's Consequence Portability and
Non-Contradiction Test rules. You can't audit a character walk against
contradiction if the destination wasn't fixed before the walk was
written. Practical use: no character should be "walked" into a new
Streetbeats crew slot without the season's endpoint for them already
decided — walking-in-progress, without a known destination, is exactly
what rule 4 warns against.

### 5. The more subtly you hide your plot points, the better

Applies to: the achievement-anchor episodes (IWD, Windrush Day) that
double as breadcrumb-planting for the Christmas Magi special. These are
explicitly designed so the payoff isn't visible until the special —
Bruk-up's arc, Fatima's early introduction, the Windrush elder's rehearsal
tune. Rule of thumb going forward: if a breadcrumb is noticeable as a
breadcrumb on first viewing, it's been planted too loudly.

### 6. A third-act problem is really a first-act problem

Applies to: Walking Policy audits generally. When a canon character's walk
into a new slot feels wrong at the point of consequence, the fix is almost
never in the scene where it breaks — it's in whether the Editorial Gate
and First-Claim Rule were properly satisfied when the character was first
established. Use this as the diagnostic question before rewriting a
resolution: what wasn't set up at the start?

### 7. Let the audience add two plus two (the Lubitsch tip)

Applies to: Coco Bredd's reveal mechanic, already locked — the slow burn
where his deeper devotion is earned through build, not stated upfront.
This is the same principle already in the brief; Wilder's phrasing is
worth keeping as the shorthand for it in future writers' notes, since
"don't do the arithmetic for them" is a quick gut-check line during
drafting.

### 8. Voice-over should add to what's seen, not describe it

Applies to: Chuck's fourth-wall-breaking direct-address role and "Chuck's
Chuck-Up." Chuck folding community feedback/fan theories back into
narration as cryptic, unattributed references is already the right
shape — the risk to guard against as the segment grows is narration
drifting into restating what's on screen rather than adding a layer the
visuals can't carry alone.

### 9. The second-act curtain event triggers the ending

Applies to: the Easter → Christmas spine directly. Carnival — the
climax, reusing the original Streetbeats sound-clash set piece, where
Reece decides — is the second-act curtain. Everything the Christmas Magi
special resolves (Bruk-up, the Windrush elder, Fatima) should trace back
to something that happens or is decided at Carnival, not to new material
introduced after it. Useful check: if the Magi special needs a plot point
that didn't originate at or before Carnival, that's a structural gap, not
a special-episode addition.

### 10. The third act builds, builds, builds — then stops

Applies to: the run from Carnival through the Christmas Magi special. The
post-Carnival Sandra/Kofi "Is Dat So?" episode pays off the
Sandra/Valentine sibling tension — that's correctly placed as rising
action, not a plateau. Discipline to hold onto as the Magi special is
scripted: once the three threads (Bruk-up, elder, Fatima) converge and
land, the season should end there. Resist the urge to add a coda scene
once the build has paid off.
