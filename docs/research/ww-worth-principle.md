## The 1955 Vote and Its Counter-Case

A concrete historical instance of comparative worth enacted as literal
policy: in 1955, when the World Health Assembly voted on whether to
include sub-Saharan Africa in the first global disease-eradication
programme in history, the decision was framed as a question of
difficulty, but functioned as a room of delegates weighing whose lives
were worth the cost of saving — and deciding, by vote, that they weren't.
Worth treated as rankable and conditional, exactly the failure mode the
principle already names.

*(This is the same 1955 WHA vote documented in `ww-guardian-physics-
cosmology.md`'s Antagonist Chemistry entry, added to this repo the same
day — cross-referencing rather than re-deriving it: 150 delegates in
Mexico City, sub-Saharan Africa excluded by name over the objections of
the small number of African delegates present, wealthier nations simply
outnumbering poorer ones on the vote.)*

A real counter-case worth keeping alongside it rather than as a separate
topic: decades later, a policy built on the opposite premise — that a
life on the other side of the world carries the same claim as one close
to home — prevented several hundred thousand deaths for a genuinely
modest cost per life. The stated reasoning behind that later policy was
explicitly framed around what a "pro-life" commitment should actually
include, once extended without a distance discount. Absolute worth,
enacted as policy rather than argued as an abstraction.

Useful as a paired example specifically because it shows the same axis
(comparative vs. absolute) playing out as real, dated, institutional
decisions on the same subject — not two different topics that happen to
rhyme.
