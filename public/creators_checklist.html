The four questions every creator must be able to answer — before anything leaves this room

In 1969, a drummer named Gregory Coleman played a four-bar break lasting seven seconds on a B-side nobody cared about. That seven seconds became the Amen Break — the most sampled drum recording in history, foundational to hip-hop, drum and bass, and jungle. Used in over 4,000 tracks. Coleman received nothing. The copyright window closed before he knew what he had made. He died in 2006, homeless and broke. Not because he lacked talent. Because nobody told him what he had made was an asset that needed protecting.

The checklist — four questions, three moments, one rule

1 Before you sign anything

Who is legally on your side right now?

  • Do you have independent legal advice? Not the label's lawyer. Not the manager's lawyer. Yours. If no — do not sign today.
  • Do you know who owns the master recording? The master is the original file. Whoever owns it controls what happens to your music forever.
  • Do you know who owns the publishing? Publishing is the song itself — the melody, the lyrics. Separate from the master. Both matter.
  • What happens to your rights if this company folds? Ask. Get the answer in writing.
  • What are the recoupable costs? Production, videos, marketing, advances — if they come out of your share first, find out exactly how much before you sign.
2 Before you upload anything

Is this documented in your name?

  • Is the recording registered with PRS for Music? prsformusic.com — free to join. Every time your music is played publicly, you are owed money. Registration is how you collect it.
  • Is it registered with PPL? ppluk.com — separate from PRS. PPL collects for the recording. PRS collects for the song. You need both.
  • Have you documented who created what? Producer, songwriter, featured artist — write it down before you upload. Disputes about this later are expensive and painful.
  • Do you own the samples you used? If you used a sample without clearance, the track is not safe to monetise. Find out before it goes live.
  • Do you have a copy of everything in a place you control? Not just the cloud. A hard drive you own. Your masters. Your contracts. Your session files.
3 Before you perform for anyone offering something

What exactly is being offered and what does it cost?

  • Is there a written agreement? Not a promise. Not a handshake. A document you can read, keep a copy of, and show to someone else.
  • What are you giving up in exchange? Exclusivity? Future releases? Rights to recordings made that day? Ask specifically. Get the answer in writing.
  • Is there a time limit on the agreement? A deal with no end date is a deal that lasts forever. Make sure there is an exit.
  • Who owns recordings made at this performance? If someone is recording — with any device — find out who owns that footage and what they can do with it.
  • What happens if they don't deliver what they promised? Every agreement should state what happens if either side breaks it.
4 Before you trust anyone with your career

Who will notice if something is going wrong?

  • Does someone you trust know what deals you have made? Not just in general. Specifically. Which label, which manager, which terms. Someone who can check up on it.
  • Do you have community around you — not just followers? People who know your actual life, not your public profile. People who will say something when something needs to be said.
  • Are you being isolated from people who care about you? Isolation is a warning sign. Managers and labels who want to separate you from your community are managing their interests, not yours.
  • Do you know where to go if something goes wrong? Musicians' Union: musiciansunion.org.uk · Intellectual Property Office: ipo.gov.uk · Citizens Advice: citizensadvice.org.uk

The one rule that overrides everything else

If you feel pressure to sign or agree to something today, right now, before you've had time to think — that pressure is the warning sign. Legitimate offers survive 48 hours. Anything that requires your signature before you can get independent advice is not an opportunity. It is a trap dressed as one. Florence Ballard signed away her rights at 24. TLC signed for 7 cents per album point as teenagers. New Edition's first manager took millions while the boys received almost nothing. The industry's oldest move is urgency. Time is always on your side. Take it.
