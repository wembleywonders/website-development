## Rationale

Reading and writing music — understanding how tempo, key, and mode shape mood and tone — used to
be foundational training for church choir and organ musicianship, and its absence is a
well-documented common weakness in modern musicianship, particularly in genres built on
ear-training and informal apprenticeship rather than notation literacy. Making this module
mandatory and first gives every Trubble n Bass member, regardless of genre, real structure for
composition rather than picking theory up incidentally alongside production work. It also gives
lyricists and composers a grounded understanding of provenance and ownership — an increasingly
urgent gap, since uncleared sampling remains a frequent source of real legal exposure for artists
and is a contributing factor in the "sameness of sound" critique often levelled at contemporary
rap and pop production.

---

## Outcome cluster 1 — Reading, writing, and hearing music

**LO1:** The member will be able to read and write standard music notation.

- 1.1 Can identify notes, rests, time signatures, and key signatures on a stave.
- 1.2 Can transcribe a short melodic phrase (played or sung) into notation.
- 1.3 Can write out an original 4–8 bar phrase in notation that another musician could play back
  accurately, without having heard the member play it first.

**LO2:** The member will understand how tempo, key, and mode shape mood and tone.

- 2.1 Can explain, using real examples from at least two Trubble n Bass genres, how tempo alone
  changes the emotional read of a piece.
- 2.2 Can identify major vs minor tonality by ear and explain the mood difference in their own
  words.
- 2.3 Can describe how a specific genre convention achieves its characteristic mood (e.g. a
  dancehall riddim's rhythmic feel, a grime beat's tempo range, a lovers rock chord voicing).

**LO3:** The member will be able to apply notation and theory to their own composition.

- 3.1 Can produce an original short composition (melody or chord progression) with accompanying
  written notation.
- 3.2 Can explain the compositional choices made (why this key, this tempo, this chord movement)
  in relation to the intended mood.

## Outcome cluster 2 — Provenance, sampling, and ownership

**LO4:** The member will understand what constitutes musical plagiarism versus legitimate
influence or clearance.

- 4.1 Can explain the legal distinction between sampling (using a portion of a recording) and
  interpolation (re-recording a melody/chord progression), and why each carries different
  clearance requirements.
- 4.2 Can describe, using at least one real documented case, what sample clearance actually
  involves and what happens when it isn't obtained. Three worked case studies (documented tier —
  see Evidence base below): *Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros. Records* (1991), *Williams v.
  Gaye* / "Blurred Lines" (2013–2018), and Black Box's "Ride on Time" (1989) featuring Loleatta
  Holloway's vocals.
- 4.3 Can explain why "it doesn't sound exactly the same" is not, on its own, a legal defence
  against a plagiarism or infringement claim.

**LO5:** The member will be able to identify provenance in their own and others' work.

- 5.1 Given a track (their own or a supplied example), can identify any sampled, interpolated, or
  heavily-referenced material and state what clearance or credit it would require.
- 5.2 Can articulate, in their own words, why over-reliance on uncleared samples or interpolated
  "feel" contributes to the "sameness of sound" critique increasingly levelled at commercial rap
  and pop — that recycling a small pool of source material narrows a genre's actual creative
  range.
- 5.3 Can propose, for their own original composition (from LO3), one deliberate way they've
  built something genuinely new rather than assembled from existing recognisable parts.

## Evidence base for LO4.2 (evidence-tiered, per Roots' documented/contested discipline)

**Documented tier — settled legal outcome:**

**Case A — *Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros. Records* (1991).** Biz Markie sampled a portion
of Gilbert O'Sullivan's "Alone Again (Naturally)" for his track "Alone Again," after his team had
already tried and failed to secure a licence, and released it anyway. The court ruled the use
willingly unlawful, opening its judgment with "Thou shalt not steal," and granted an injunction
halting the album's distribution. The court explicitly noted that the prior failed licence
attempt helped establish the infringement was knowing and intentional — trying and failing to
clear a sample and using it regardless makes the case worse, not better. Widely regarded as the
ruling that ended sampling's legal gray area and reshaped the hip-hop industry's relationship to
clearance going forward. Best case for teaching LO4.3 directly.

**Case C — Black Box, "Ride on Time" (1989) / Loleatta Holloway v. Black Box.** Black Box built
"Ride on Time" almost entirely from an uncleared sample of Loleatta Holloway's 1980 vocal "Love
Sensation" (the title is itself taken from a Holloway lyric — "thank you baby, 'cause you're
right on time"). Neither Holloway nor the song's co-writer/producer Dan Hartman were consulted or
credited. Black Box hired a model, Katrin Quinol, to mime the vocals on Top of the Pops and in
the video, presenting her to the public as the singer while Holloway's real voice carried the
entire track — a genuine parallel to Milli Vanilli, but layered on top of an uncleared sample
rather than a fully faked recording. The song was the UK's best-selling single of 1989 (six weeks
at number one) before Holloway and Hartman sued and won an undisclosed settlement; Black Box had
to reissue the track with new vocals from a then-unknown Heather Small. The same production team
used a second uncredited Black vocalist, Martha Wash, elsewhere on the same album with a
different model lip-syncing in her place — establishing this as a documented pattern of that
era's dance-music industry, not an isolated incident. The case is credited as a direct
contributor to later US legislation requiring mandatory vocal credits on albums and videos. Best
case for teaching the identity/credit-erasure dimension of provenance, beyond pure financial
harm.

**Contested tier — real legal outcome, genuinely disputed reasoning:**

**Case B — *Williams v. Gaye* / "Blurred Lines" (2013–2018).** Pharrell Williams and Robin
Thicke's "Blurred Lines" was found to infringe Marvin Gaye's "Got to Give It Up" — not through
sampling, but through similarity of "feel" and style. No melody, lyrics, or recorded audio was
copied. A jury found infringement anyway, awarding the largest damages figure in music-copyright
history at the time; a federal appeals court upheld the verdict 2–1, with the dissenting judge
arguing the two songs differed in melody, harmony, and rhythm, and that the ruling effectively let
the Gaye estate copyright an entire musical style. Over 200 artists, including members of Hall &
Oates, The Black Crowes, and Tears for Fears, publicly opposed the precedent. Present this case
explicitly as contested, not settled consensus — the legal boundary between influence and
infringement remains a live debate, and members should understand that "feel"-based infringement
is a real but disputed area of the law, not a bright line like Case A.

**Sourcing note:** All three cases are drawn from court records, contemporaneous reporting, and
documented interviews with the participants (including Black Box's own producers reflecting on
the case decades later) — not music-industry folklore. Case B in particular should be taught with
its dissent and industry pushback intact, consistent with Roots' evidence-tiering discipline of
distinguishing documented from contested claims.

## Assessment method

A portfolio submission containing: (a) the notated original composition from LO3; (b) a short
written reflection (300–500 words) covering the mood/tempo/key reasoning behind it; (c) a
provenance statement identifying any influences, samples, or referenced material in the member's
own work and how each would be legally handled; and (d) a short written response (200–300 words)
analysing one of the three case studies above and what it demonstrates about clearance, credit,
or the influence/infringement boundary.

## Internal verification notes (NOT part of any external submission — for WW's own record only)

- **The three legal cases were fact-checked directly via web search, not taken on trust — all
  three are substantively accurate.**
  - *Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros.*: confirmed against Wikipedia, Justia, and Quimbee case
    summaries — Judge Kevin Duffy, the "Thou shalt not steal" opening, the prior failed licence
    attempt, and the injunction are all correct.
  - *Williams v. Gaye*: confirmed against the 9th Circuit's own published opinion and multiple law
    firm case summaries — the jury verdict, damages, the 2–1 appellate split, and Judge Nguyen's
    dissent (differing melody/harmony/rhythm, the "copyrighting a style" warning) all check out.
  - Black Box / Loleatta Holloway: confirmed against NME's 30th-anniversary retrospective and
    Wikipedia — the uncleared "Love Sensation" sample, Katrin Quinol miming, the lawsuit and
    settlement, Heather Small's re-recording, and Martha Wash's uncredited parallel vocals on the
    same album are all correct.
  - **Two secondary claims not independently re-verified, flagged rather than cut:** the specific
    superlative "largest damages figure in music-copyright history *at the time*" (plausible,
    consistent with the scale found, not specifically confirmed as a formal record); and Black
    Box's case as "a direct contributor to later US legislation requiring mandatory vocal
    credits" (plausible — the search results explicitly describe it as "a genuine parallel to
    Milli Vanilli," and Milli Vanilli's scandal is the case more commonly credited with prompting
    that legislative response; this document's causal claim specifically about Black Box wasn't
    independently confirmed).
  - **The document's own documented/contested tiering is well-calibrated against the real legal
    landscape** — Cases A and C both reached clear, undisputed outcomes (injunction, settlement);
    Case B is genuinely contested (a split appellate ruling plus organised industry pushback).
    This isn't a case of internal WW lore dressed up as legal fact — it holds up.
